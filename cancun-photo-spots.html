Read This First

Your resort beach is rarely the right beach

Most guides to Cancún photo spots are written by people who have never had to make a photograph there at four in the afternoon. They list the postcard and skip the part that decides whether the session works: which direction the sand faces, when the light stops being harsh, whether a bus of day visitors arrives at ten, and whether that stretch is under seaweed this month.

This page is the other kind of guide. Every spot below carries the hour it photographs best and the rule that governs it. Some of the most photogenic places on this coast are also the most regulated: archaeological zones charge for professional equipment, some beaches are federal property and free to anyone, and a handful of resorts will not let an outside camera past the lobby.

We scout the specific stretch before every session, because the honest answer to where should we shoot depends on your dates, your resort and where the seaweed is that week.

Inside Cancún

The beaches that hold up on camera

The Hotel Zone runs roughly north to south along Boulevard Kukulcán, and that shape decides everything. The northern stretch faces west across the lagoon side and the long eastern strand takes the sunrise full on.

01

Playa Delfines, sunrise only

The wide public beach at kilometre 18, high dune, open horizon, no buildings in frame. East facing, so it belongs to sunrise and to nobody else after nine. Free, public and busy by mid morning.

02

Punta Norte and the north shore

The short northern stretch near Punta Cancún faces the calmer bay. Softer water, gentler entry, and the only part of the Hotel Zone that can hold a sunset with the sun behind you rather than at your back.

03

Costa Mujeres, the quiet north

Twenty minutes past the Hotel Zone, wide sand and far fewer people. Worth the drive for family sessions where a crowd would break the frame. Sunrise here is unhurried.

04

Puerto Morelos, fishing village calm

South of the airport, a reef town with coloured boats and a leaning lighthouse. Small enough to feel private, close enough for a golden hour session without a long drive back.

05

Isla Mujeres, thirty minutes by ferry

Punta Sur cliffs at sunrise, Playa Norte for the shallow turquoise. The ferry schedule, not the light, is what usually decides the timeline here.

06

The lagoon side at sunset

Nichupté faces west, which is why every sunset frame that shows the sun itself was made on the lagoon and not the sea. Good for couples, poor for swimming.

Beyond the City

Cenotes, ruins and the Riviera Maya

01

Cenotes, and the light shaft myth

The famous beam at Suytun or Dos Ojos exists for a narrow window near midday, and only on a clear day. Everything else in a cenote is low, cool and slow. Bring patience, expect an entrance fee per person, and check whether the operator allows professional gear before you drive two hours.

02

Tulum ruins and archaeological zones

INAH sites are open to visitors with a phone, but professional cameras, tripods and anything that looks commercial require a permit applied for in advance and paid per day. It is not expensive, it is just not spontaneous. Plan it weeks out or choose the beach below the ruins instead.

03

Akumal, Xpu-Ha and the quiet bays

South of Playa del Carmen the coastline softens into bays with calm water and palms that frame without effort. Xpu-Ha stays the least trafficked of the three and photographs beautifully in the last hour.

04

Mayakoba and the resort canals

Inside the resort corridor, mangrove canals and boardwalks give a different vocabulary from open sand. Access depends on being a guest, and most properties charge an outside photographer fee.

05

Playa Mamitas and central Playa del Carmen

Walkable, lively, and best treated as a backdrop rather than a private stage. Early morning is the only hour it is quiet.

06

Bacalar, if you have a whole day

The lagoon of seven colours is three hours each way from Cancún. Extraordinary, and only worth it if the trip is built around it rather than squeezed into an afternoon.

The Practical Part

Hours, permits and seaweed

Three things decide whether a location works on your date, and none of them appear in the postcard.

Golden hour moves through the year

In winter the good light runs roughly from 5:30 to 6:30 in the evening, in summer closer to 6:30 to 7:30. Sunrise is the mirror. We build the timeline backwards from that window, never from what is convenient.

Sargassum has a season

Seaweed lands mainly between April and August, and it does not land evenly: the east facing beaches take it first and the north shore, Isla Mujeres and the lagoon side often stay clear. We check the current week before choosing, not the average.

Permits belong to certain places

Archaeological zones need a professional photography permit paid per day. Public beaches are federal and free. Resorts set their own rules and many charge an outside vendor fee to photographers not on their list.

Public means public

The beach in Mexico belongs to the nation up to the tide line, so nobody can charge you for standing on the sand. What a hotel can control is the access through its property.

Straight Answers

What people ask about locations

What is the best photo spot in Cancún?

For sunrise, Playa Delfines: wide, high and open, with no buildings in frame. For sunset with the sun actually visible, the lagoon side, because the Hotel Zone beach faces east. For a session that has to stay calm and uncrowded, Costa Mujeres or Puerto Morelos. There is no single best spot, there is the right spot for your hour and your date.

Do I need a permit to take photos on the beach in Cancún?

Not on a public beach. Mexican beaches are federal property up to the tide line, so no hotel or business can charge you for photographing there. Archaeological zones are different: professional cameras and tripods require a permit paid per day, applied for in advance.

Can we photograph at our resort?

Usually yes as a guest, but many properties in the Hotel Zone charge an outside vendor fee to photographers not on their in-house list, paid by you to the hotel. We confirm your resort's current policy before the session so it is never a surprise.

When is the seaweed season?

Sargassum arrives mainly between April and August and hits the east facing beaches hardest. The north shore, Isla Mujeres and the lagoon side often stay clear even in a heavy week. We check current conditions rather than the calendar average.

What time should we photograph?

The first or the last hour of daylight, always. In winter that is roughly 5:30 to 6:30 in the evening and in summer closer to 6:30 to 7:30. Midday on this coast is straight overhead light: it flattens faces and burns the water white.

Are cenote photos worth the drive?

For couples, often yes. For families with small children, rarely: the light is low, the ground is slippery and the drive can be two hours each way. If a cenote matters to you, we build the day around it instead of squeezing it in.

Can you photograph us on Isla Mujeres?

Yes, and it is one of the best options for calm turquoise water. The ferry schedule sets the timeline, so we plan around the crossing rather than around the light alone.
