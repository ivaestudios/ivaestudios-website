Most money advice for Cancun assumes US dollars. Fly in from Canada, perhaps on one of this winter's new routes, and the question changes: Cancun does not run on Canadian cash, US dollars would be a second foreign currency for you, and your card may add a charge of its own. We checked the rules on official Canadian and Mexican pages, and on Scotiabank's and Visa's own pages, on November 14, 2026.

Can You Pay in Canadian Dollars? Plan on Pesos

Mexico's currency is the peso, and Article 8 of its Monetary Law says foreign currency is not legal tender there. A taxi, a beach club or a hotel that takes Canadian dollars does so by choice, at its own rate. Canada's travel advice adds a wrinkle: Mexico limits how many US dollars residents and foreigners can exchange, and while that rule does not apply to Canadian dollars, some financial institutions, hotels and exchange bureaus don't make the distinction.

So, do Cancun resorts accept Canadian dollars? That is each resort's call; ask the front desk before you count on it. The simpler plan: a card for most things, pesos for the small ones, and your Canadian cash left for the ride home.

Pesos Beat US Dollars: One Conversion, Not Two

For an American, paying in US dollars skips a conversion. For a Canadian, it adds one. Buy US cash at home and you pay a cash exchange rate, which Scotiabank says is a little higher than the non-cash rate because cash costs more to handle; spend it in Cancun and the vendor turns it into pesos at a rate it sets. Two conversions, two margins. Pay the same peso price by card and your bank does the converting, at its own rate, plus its conversion charge if your card has one. The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC) notes that financial institutions may convert some purchases to US dollars first and then to Canadian dollars; even then, the rates are your bank's, not a vendor's.

The same goes for a shop that offers to turn a peso total into US dollars for your card. The rule: pay in the currency of the price. To judge any rate you are offered, start from the central banks' reference rates.

The Card Features That Matter

No foreign currency conversion charge. FCAC explains that when you use a credit card outside Canada, your financial institution applies an exchange rate and a foreign currency conversion charge, calculated after converting the purchase to Canadian dollars. Its worked example uses 2.5%, and Scotiabank calls 2.5% the mark-up card issuers typically charge. Some Canadian cards waive it; your card agreement says which kind you hold.

A debit card for cash. FCAC warns that a credit card cash advance outside Canada typically carries a higher fee than at home and runs interest from the day you take the money. Debit cards work in most countries, it says; which ATMs you can use depends on your bank's network, such as Visa's Plus or Mastercard's Cirrus and Maestro, and some ATMs accept only 4-digit PINs.

A backup and a heads-up. Carry a second way to pay, and tell your bank you are traveling so it doesn't freeze your card, as FCAC suggests. Canada's advice for Mexico adds: avoid giving your card to bar or restaurant staff, and avoid paying by card inside a taxi.

When the Screen Offers Canadian Dollars, Say Pesos

Some terminals and ATMs offer to bill you in Canadian dollars instead of pesos. Visa calls this dynamic currency conversion and says paying in your home currency includes an exchange rate and additional fees. Visa requires the screen to show both amounts, the rate and any markup; merchants and ATMs should give you the choice and must not choose for you, and declining does not stop you from paying or withdrawing cash.

On every screen

Choose pesos and let your own bank do the conversion. If the screen hides the rate or you feel pushed, Visa recommends declining the offer and reporting it to your card issuer.

Cash: A Bank ATM, Not the Airport

Canada's travel advice is direct: avoid withdrawing or exchanging money in public areas of the airport, use ATMs in public areas or inside a bank or business, cover the keypad and don't withdraw large sums. If your bank sells pesos, a small amount bought before you fly costs the cash rate but spares you the arrivals hall.

Fees come in layers. FCAC lists a withdrawal fee from your own account, a network access fee your bank may charge when you use an ATM it doesn't own and a convenience fee from the ATM's owner, and warns that you may pay more fees at ATMs outside Canada; its debit card guide adds that foreign currency conversion fees may apply to withdrawals abroad. In Mexico, the financial services transparency law requires ATM operators to show their fee on screen and get your consent before charging it: read it, and cancel if you don't accept it.

Bank with Scotiabank? Use the ATMs of its Mexican partner in the Global ATM Alliance, Grupo Financiero Scotiabank Inverlat. Scotiabank says Alliance members waive surcharges on withdrawals with a ScotiaCard or Scotiabank Visa and that it waives its international ATM access fee on select accounts; its 2.5% foreign exchange fee still applies. Other banks set their own terms.

The Airport Checklist

  • Cards. The one without the 2.5% charge if you have it, a debit card for cash and a backup. Tell your bank you are traveling; check that your PIN has 4 digits.
  • Pesos. Small bills for the first day's tips, snacks and taxis, bought at your bank if it sells them.
  • Arrivals. No withdrawals or exchanges in the airport's public areas. Paying a taxi by card? Canada's advice is to book and pre-pay inside the terminal or at the designated boarding area.
  • In town. An ATM inside a bank branch, or Scotiabank Inverlat for Scotiabank clients. Read the fee, then choose pesos.
  • Flying home. Spend your coins: Scotiabank notes that foreign coins can't be exchanged in Canada.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use Canadian dollars in Cancun?

Plan not to. Foreign currency is not legal tender in Mexico, so a business that takes Canadian dollars does so by choice, at its own rate. Use pesos and a card.

Should I pay in pesos or US dollars with a Canadian card?

Pesos, when the price is in pesos, and your bank converts at its own rate. If a vendor converts the price to US dollars first, you pay the vendor's margin and then your bank's.

What is the best kind of card for Mexico from Canada?

One without a foreign currency conversion charge, which Scotiabank describes as typically 2.5%. Use a debit card for cash: FCAC says a credit card cash advance outside Canada typically carries a higher fee and charges interest from the day you take the money.

What ATM fees will I pay in Cancun with a Canadian card?

Possibly several: the ATM operator's fee, which Mexican law requires the screen to show before you agree; your own bank's network access and withdrawal fees; and a currency conversion fee. With a ScotiaCard or Scotiabank Visa, Scotiabank says the surcharge is waived at Scotiabank Inverlat ATMs.

Should I exchange money at the Cancun airport?

Canada's travel advice says to avoid withdrawing or exchanging money in public areas of the airport. Arrive with some pesos in small bills and use an ATM inside a bank branch in town.

How do I pay for a family session with IVAE Studios?

At the end of the session, by card or cash, with no deposit. Collections are priced in US dollars, from 295 to 895 USD; The Chapter, recommended for families, is 475 USD.
