Every big family photo has an organizer: the person who started the group chat, booked the rooms or cares most that the picture happens. If that is you, these large group photography tips are for you. An extended family of ten to sixteen, three generations, a baby on a nap schedule and a teenager who would rather be at the pool can all fit into a relaxed 60 to 90 minutes. Most of what makes it relaxed is decided before anyone reaches the sand.

One Organizer, One Conversation

Sixteen people bring sixteen opinions about the time, the place and the shirts. Choose one person to talk to us and let everyone else talk to that person. One thread means nothing gets lost and decisions get made. Collect four answers in the family chat by a deadline, then send them to us in one message:

  • Headcount, counting anyone who lands late or flies home early.
  • Ages, from the baby to the eldest grandparent: the two ends of the family set the pace.
  • Must-have groupings, written out with names, one line per photo, most important first.
  • Mobility needs, privately. If someone tires quickly or uses a cane, a walker or a wheelchair, message us one to one; nobody is singled out in front of the family.

Written down before the trip, the groupings no longer depend on anyone's memory on the day, and your list becomes our running order. If a grandparent's mobility shapes the plan, see family photos with less mobile grandparents.

Agree on a Palette, Not a Uniform

Identical T-shirts read as a team photo. What ties 16 people together is a shared palette: two or three colors that sit well together, each household choosing its own pieces within them. Post it early in the family chat with one reference image and a date by which every outfit is packed. Before the session we send guidance on what to wear; the organizer forwards it to every household. As one client put it:

“Vianey and her team sent us really helpful guidance on what to wear, the scenes we'd shoot and how the session would run”

Claire L., Google review

If your collection includes two looks, decide in advance who changes: one household changing is quick, sixteen people changing at once is not. The Day-Of Checklist

On the day, what unsettles a big group is rarely the camera: a missing sandal, a buzzing phone, a hungry four-year-old. Share out these five jobs the day before:

  1. Dressed and at the meeting point 15 minutes before the start. In a group of 16, the last person to arrive sets the start time for everyone, and the light does not wait.
  2. Phones, sunglasses and bags in one tote, held by one person out of frame. Empty pockets photograph better, and nobody checks a message mid-portrait.
  3. Water and snacks for the children and the grandparents, nothing that stains a white shirt, kept out of sight until a break.
  4. Decide who holds the baby in each grouping, a parent for the household portrait and a grandparent for the three generations, so nobody spends minutes passing the baby around.
  5. Let households with small children leave in waves. Tell us who needs to go first and we photograph their groupings early, so toddlers are not stuck waiting through every other frame.

Why the Full Group Comes First

It is tempting to save the big photo for the end. We take it first, because it is the only frame that needs all sixteen people at once, and every minute makes that harder: a toddler's patience runs out, a grandparent needs to sit, a cousin disappears. So the organizer's deadline is minute one, with everyone there and nobody still changing. Once the group frame exists, the pressure is off, and we work down your list: each household, the grandparents with the grandchildren, the siblings, the cousins.

Prompts That Get Real Expressions From a Crowd

Ask sixteen people to smile and you get sixteen polite versions of the same smile, plus one blink. Give them one small task to do together and the faces change. Four prompts that work for a big family photoshoot:

“Everyone, look at the youngest child.”

Every face softens at once, because people look at a small child in a way they never look at a lens. It is the natural prompt for a three-generation family photo.

“Whisper something to the person on your right.”

Nobody knows what anyone else said, so the laughter travels down the line. Even teenagers who will not smile on command tend to crack.

“Walk and talk.”

The family walks slowly toward the camera, arm in arm, talking instead of posing. Shoulders loosen, the children run ahead, and it looks like your family, not a lineup.

“Grandparents, tell us a family story.”

How they met, or the first family trip. Children and grandchildren listen, and the reactions are real because the story is.

The organizer can help: tell us the youngest child's name, a nickname that makes the cousins laugh, the story grandma loves to tell. Nobody needs to rehearse. Direction is our job, in English and Spanish, which matters when relatives do not share a first language.

When a Child Says No

In a group this size, someone small may decide halfway through that they are done. That is normal. What helps is what the adults do next: no bribes shouted across the sand, no countdowns, no apologies to the rest of the family. A parent steps aside with the child, we keep working with everyone else, and the child's frames happen later or as they are, sometimes on a parent's hip. Frequently Asked Questions

How do you organize family photos for a large group?

Name one organizer to talk to the photographer, and before the trip send the headcount, the ages, the must-have groupings and any mobility needs. Agree on a palette, arrive dressed 15 minutes early, give one person the phones and bags, and bring water and snacks. The full group is photographed first.

What are good prompts for big family photos?

Give everyone the same small task at once: look at the youngest child, whisper something to the person on your right, walk and talk toward the camera, or listen while the grandparents tell a family story. Real expressions follow.

How long should a photo session for 10 to 16 people be?

Plan on 60 to 90 minutes, because every household adds frames. The Chapter is 60 minutes for 475 USD and The Story 75 minutes for 545 USD. With 14 to 16 people, we suggest The Editorial, 645 USD, or The Heirloom, 895 USD, both 90 minutes.

Do you charge per person for large family photos?

No. There is no per-person charge and no limit on the number of people, so a collection costs the same for four people or sixteen. There is no deposit either: you pay at the end of the session, and cancellation is free until one day before.

Can you arrange transportation for a group of 16?

Yes. For groups of up to 16 people, we can arrange transportation in a full van to take everyone to the session location. The transport price is quoted on request, so mention it in your first message.
