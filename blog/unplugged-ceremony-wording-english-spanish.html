Unplugged ceremonies stopped being a novelty a while ago. In 2026 they are close to the default among couples who care about their photographs, and at a Mexico destination wedding the request comes with a wrinkle nobody writes about: half your guests read English and half read Spanish, and the wording that works in one language falls flat or sounds officious in the other. This is the wording we have watched work in front of real bilingual guest lists, plus an honest look at what the ceremony frames actually gain.

Why Couples Go Unplugged, and What It Fixes

Ask a couple why they want an unplugged ceremony and the reason is almost never photographic. It is that they want to look out at faces. They spent a year planning, their people flew across a continent, and they would like to see eyes rather than the backs of phones during the twenty five minutes that the whole thing is actually about.

The photographic benefit is real but narrower than the internet claims. Most of a wedding day is unaffected by guest phones. Getting ready, cocktail hour, dinner, the party, the portraits: phones are fine and often add something. The ceremony is different for one structural reason. During the ceremony the guests are stationary, seated in rows on either side of a narrow aisle, and the photographer is working from a small number of fixed positions. There is nowhere to move to. A phone raised in row three is in the frame, permanently, for the entire processional.

What we tell couples who are unsure

Ask for the ceremony only. Nothing before, nothing after. The request is short, specific and easy to honor, and a guest who is told the reception is wide open complies happily. Requests that extend across the whole day get ignored by the third hour and make everyone feel managed.

Aisle Shots With Phones Versus Without

Here is what actually changes, frame by frame, described plainly by the people who have to make those frames.

The processional. This is the single most affected image of the day. We shoot it from the front, low, down the length of the aisle, with the person walking centred and the rows falling away on both sides. Guests who film lean into the aisle to get their angle, and their arm and phone enter the frame at exactly the height of the walking person's face. On a narrow beach aisle, one leaning guest at row two can compromise every frame of a forty second walk. Without phones, the rows stay in their seats, the aisle stays clean, and the faces on both sides are turned toward the person walking with expressions you would frame.

The first kiss. The wide version of this frame includes the guests behind the couple. With phones, that background is a row of glowing rectangles and faces obscured by hands. Without them, it is a row of open faces mid-reaction. Same kiss, entirely different photograph.

The recessional. Guests filming stay seated to hold their shot steady. Guests who are not filming stand up, cheer and throw their hands in the air. That is the difference between a documentary frame and a celebration frame, and it is decided months earlier by a sentence on a sign.

The father, mother or friend at the end of the row. The most painful one. A parent who films the entire processional on a phone appears in your gallery with a device in front of their face, and never actually saw it happen with their own eyes. We have had more than one parent tell us afterward that they wished someone had asked them to put it down.

"You are not asking guests to stop taking pictures. You are asking them to be in the room for twenty five minutes."

Sign Wording in English and Spanish

One sign, both languages, at the entrance to the ceremony space where guests pass before they sit. Not at the welcome table, not at the bar. Here are three versions by tone. Copy them directly.

Warm and simple. This is the one we recommend most often, and the Spanish is written the way a Mexican guest would say it rather than translated word for word.

Sign, warm version

Welcome. We have chosen an unplugged ceremony.
Please keep phones and cameras away until after the kiss, and be here with us. Our photographer will capture everything, and we will share it all with you.

Bienvenidos. Nuestra ceremonia es sin celulares ni cámaras.
Guarda tu teléfono hasta después del beso y vive el momento con nosotros. Nuestra fotógrafa lo está capturando todo y después lo compartimos contigo.

Short and elegant. For couples with minimal signage and a formal aesthetic, where a long paragraph would look wrong on a calligraphed board.

Sign, short version

An unplugged ceremony. Please, no phones or cameras.
Be present with us. The photos are on us.

Ceremonia sin celulares. Por favor, sin teléfonos ni cámaras.
Vive el momento con nosotros. De las fotos nos encargamos nosotros.

Playful. For a relaxed beach wedding where the rest of the signage has humour in it. Humour translates badly, so the Spanish here is a parallel joke rather than the same one.

Sign, playful version

We hired a professional. She is very good. Please put the phones away and enjoy the show.

Ya contratamos a una profesional y es buenísima. Guarda el celular y disfruta la función.

Two production notes from experience. Put the English above the Spanish or side by side at equal size, never the Spanish in smaller type underneath, because guests notice and it reads as an afterthought. And keep the total word count low: a sign that takes fifteen seconds to read gets read by nobody at a beach entrance with a breeze and a drink in hand.

Website and Invitation Wording for Both Languages

The sign is a reminder. The wedding website is where the request is actually made, because that is where guests are when they have time to absorb it, usually months before travel. Put it on the schedule page next to the ceremony time, not buried in an FAQ.

Website paragraph, English

A note about our ceremony. We have chosen to keep it unplugged, which simply means we would love you to put phones and cameras away from the moment the music starts until we walk back down the aisle together. You have travelled a long way to be with us and we want to see your faces, not screens. Our photographer will be documenting every moment, and the full gallery goes to all of you afterward. Once the ceremony ends, photograph everything. Please.

Website paragraph, Spanish

Una nota sobre la ceremonia. Decidimos que sea sin celulares ni cámaras. Es decir, te pedimos guardar el teléfono desde que empieza la música hasta que salgamos juntos del pasillo. Viajaste mucho para acompañarnos y queremos ver tu cara, no una pantalla. Nuestra fotógrafa va a documentar todo y después les compartimos la galería completa. Al terminar la ceremonia, toma todas las fotos que quieras. Por favor.

What We Ask Your Officiant to Say, Word for Word

This is the part that does the work. Signs and websites prepare people. The spoken announcement, delivered about sixty seconds before the processional starts while everyone is already seated and quiet, is what actually moves the phones into pockets. We ask every officiant and celebrant we work with to deliver it, and we ask them to do it in both languages if the guest list is mixed.

Officiant announcement, English

Good evening, everyone, and welcome. Before we begin, a small request from the couple. They have asked for an unplugged ceremony, so please take a moment now to silence your phone and put it fully away. For the next twenty minutes, be right here with them. There is a wonderful photographer working, every moment is being captured, and you will all receive the pictures. After the kiss, take as many photos as you like. Thank you.

Officiant announcement, Spanish

Buenas tardes a todos y bienvenidos. Antes de comenzar, una petición de los novios. Nos pidieron que la ceremonia sea sin celulares, así que te pedimos silenciar tu teléfono y guardarlo por completo. Durante los próximos veinte minutos, está aquí con ellos. Hay una fotógrafa profesional trabajando, todo se está capturando y todos van a recibir las fotos. Después del beso, toma todas las que quieras. Gracias.

Three details make this land. Ask them to say it after everyone is seated, not while people are still arriving. Ask for the words "put it fully away", because "silence your phone" alone produces a row of silent phones held at eye level. And ask them to name the after, explicitly, because that sentence is what turns a restriction into a deal.

The Hybrid: Unplugged Ceremony, Open Reception

Nearly every couple we work with lands here, and it is the right answer. The ceremony is unrepeatable, tightly framed and easy to ruin. The reception is chaotic by design, and guest phones genuinely add to it. Group selfies at the table, the dance floor from angles we are not standing at, the ten second video of the abuela dancing that ends up in the family group chat for a decade.

Say the two halves together every time you communicate the request. On the sign, on the website, in the officiant's announcement. The pairing is what makes it generous. A couple who says "no phones during the ceremony, and then please photograph absolutely everything" gets near total compliance. A couple who only says the first half gets grumbling.

How to Say It to 80 Travelling Guests Without Sounding Rude

A destination wedding guest has spent money and vacation days to be there. That changes the emotional maths of asking them for anything, and it is why unplugged wording that works fine at a hometown wedding can land badly in Cancún. Four principles keep it warm.

  • Give the reason before the rule. "We want to see your faces" first, "please put phones away" second. Reversed, it reads as policy. In that order, it reads as affection.
  • Thank them for travelling, in the same breath. One clause acknowledging the trip transforms the tone of the whole paragraph, in both languages.
  • Promise the photos, and then actually deliver them. Guests comply because they trust the pictures are coming. We deliver private galleries in one to three business days, and couples who share that link quickly find guests remember the unplugged ceremony fondly rather than as a restriction.
  • Never make the officiant scold. If a guest lifts a phone anyway, nothing happens. No one is corrected in front of eighty people. We work around one phone easily. The announcement exists to move ninety five percent of them, not to enforce a hundred.

One last note on the Spanish. Mexican wedding culture is warm, familial and slightly more formal in its written register than English-language wedding copy. Direct translations of American unplugged signage often come out either brusque or oddly stiff. If your planner or stationer is not a native speaker, have someone read the Spanish out loud before it goes to print. We do this for our couples as a matter of course, because the studio works in both languages every week.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does an unplugged ceremony mean?
It means guests are asked to put phones, tablets and cameras away for the ceremony itself, usually from the moment the processional begins until the recessional ends. Nothing is confiscated and nobody is policed. It is a request, communicated in advance and repeated once out loud, and in our experience compliance is close to total when it is asked for clearly and warmly.
How do you say unplugged ceremony in Spanish?
There is no single accepted translation, and the literal one does not work. The wording that reads naturally to Mexican and Latin American guests is "ceremonia sin cámaras ni celulares" or, more warmly, "una ceremonia para vivirla, no para grabarla". On signage we normally use "Ceremonia sin celulares" as the headline with a softer line beneath it, because it is instantly understood and does not sound like a rule from an airline.
Does an unplugged ceremony really improve the photos?
In three specific frames, dramatically. The processional shot down the aisle, where a raised phone at the end of a row lands directly between the camera and the person walking. The first kiss, where a wall of screens appears behind the couple in every wide frame. And the recessional, where guests filming stay seated and looking at screens instead of standing and reacting. Everything else in the day is largely unaffected, which is why we never ask couples to extend the request past the ceremony.
Is it rude to ask destination wedding guests to put their phones away?
Not if you explain why and thank them for it. Guests who flew to Mexico for you want to be part of the day, and framing the request around presence rather than prohibition converts almost everyone. The wording that causes friction is the wording that sounds like an instruction. The wording that works sounds like an invitation, and it says explicitly that photos are welcome again straight after the ceremony.
Where should the unplugged sign go?
One sign at the point where guests enter the ceremony space, at eye level, before they sit down. A second small card on each seat is optional and helps at large weddings. Signs placed at the bar, at the welcome table or anywhere before the ceremony area get read and forgotten. For a bilingual guest list, put both languages on the same sign rather than making two signs, so nobody feels the message was not meant for them.
Can guests take photos at the reception if the ceremony is unplugged?
Yes, and we encourage couples to say so out loud. The hybrid approach, an unplugged ceremony and a fully open reception, is what almost all of our couples choose. It protects the twenty five minutes that cannot be repeated and leaves the rest of the night to the guests, their phones and their group chats. Saying it clearly in the officiant's announcement is what makes the request land as generous rather than restrictive.
