What the Groom Should Wear to a Beach Wedding

In short

Choose linen-cotton blends, silk-linen or high-twist tropical wool: a late afternoon ceremony from May through September can start at 31 to 34 degrees Celsius, and heavy wool above 300 grams, pure linen and non-breathing polyester all fail in that heat. Then plan the last 30 minutes before the ceremony, which decide the first twenty photographs.

Almost every what-to-wear guide for a Mexican beach wedding is written for the bride and for the guests. The wedding party gets a sentence. That is backwards, because attire decisions on this coast do not only affect how the men feel at 32 degrees with the humidity of a July afternoon, they decide what the whole gallery looks like. A neon shirt throws coloured light onto everybody's skin. Heavy wool goes shapeless by five. Cheap white linen goes see-through the moment the sun sits behind it. Here is what we tell grooms who ask, from a studio that photographs this exact light every week.

Heat and Humidity: the Fabric Shortlist

Quintana Roo runs warm all year and genuinely hot from May through September, when a late afternoon ceremony can start at 31 to 34 degrees Celsius with humidity high enough that the air feels heavier than the temperature suggests. Fabric choice is therefore not a style question first, it is a comfort question with a style consequence.

The shortlist is short. Linen is the classic answer for good reason: it is breathable, it moves, and it photographs with a texture that reads as expensive even in a mid-priced suit. Linen-cotton blends give you most of the breathability with far better crease recovery. Cotton seersucker is excellent, since the puckered weave holds the cloth off your skin and lets air move underneath. High-twist tropical wool, the open-weave cloth tailors call fresco, is the dark-horse choice: it looks like a proper suit, breathes far better than people expect and resists creasing much more than linen does. Silk-linen blends give a soft sheen at golden hour that photographs beautifully.

What is not on the list is anything synthetic. Polyester and poly blends, which is what most rented suits and a lot of inexpensive groomsmen packages are made of, do not breathe at all, so the heat has nowhere to go. They also have a specific problem on camera: synthetic cloth reflects light with a hard, plastic sheen that low sun exaggerates, and it renders the same suit shinier in photographs than it looked in the room. Heavy worsted wool above roughly 300 grams and anything velvet belong to a different climate entirely.

Treat the jacket as optional

The best move most grooms make is planning for the jacket to come off. Wear it for the ceremony and the formal portraits, then take it off for cocktail hour and the reception. That means the shirt underneath has to be good on its own: proper fit through the shoulders, no visible undershirt through thin cloth, sleeves that look intentional rolled. Plan the shirt as an outfit, not as a layer nobody will see.

Colours That Photograph Well Against Turquoise

The backdrop here is fixed: turquoise water, pale sand, a low warm sun. Everything you wear is judged against those three, and warm neutrals win every time. Sand, oatmeal, bone, stone, tan, camel and soft ivory all sit next to Caribbean water without fighting it, and they read as light rather than as an absence of colour. They also reflect heat instead of absorbing it, which matters at four in the afternoon.

Beyond neutrals, three families work reliably. Muted sage and soft olive against sand look considered rather than trendy. Dusty or French blue is lovely, with one caveat: a blue too close to the water reads as camouflage in wide frames, so go either clearly lighter or clearly deeper than the sea rather than matching it. Terracotta, clay and warm rust are underused here and they glow at golden hour, because the sun at that hour is already warm and the fabric agrees with it.

Two cautions. Pure black absorbs heat, which is the practical objection, and it has an optical one too: at golden hour, with the sun behind you, black loses all its detail and becomes a flat shape against a bright sky. Navy does the same job while keeping some structure, so if you want dark, go navy or a deep charcoal-brown. And be careful about ivory if the bride is in ivory. It can be exquisite when it is a deliberate tonal choice and awkward when it is an accident, so decide on purpose.

Colours and Fabrics That Go Transparent or Wrinkle by Five

Now the failures, which are more useful than the successes because they are avoidable.

Transparency is the one nobody plans for. Thin white or pale linen trousers, photographed with a low sun behind you, become translucent, and dark underwear turns into the most memorable detail of the frame. The fix is easy and takes one minute: wear underwear close to your own skin tone rather than black, and check for lining when you buy. Hold the trousers up to a bright window in the shop. If you can see your hand through them, you will see everything at golden hour. The same is true of very thin white shirts over a dark undershirt.

Creasing is the second. Pure linen wrinkles by design, and a 100 percent linen suit that looked crisp at four in the afternoon will look thoroughly lived in by the time you sit through a two-hour dinner. Plenty of grooms genuinely like that, and it suits a relaxed beach wedding. If you do not, choose a blend rather than fighting the fabric. And whichever you pick, hang it in air conditioning the night before, put the jacket on last, and try not to sit in it any longer than necessary before the ceremony. A car seat sets a crease across the back of a linen jacket in twenty minutes.

Third, saturated colour. A bright red, electric blue or neon shirt on someone standing next to you does not stay on that person. Strong sunlight bounces off it and casts that colour onto adjacent skin, and a face lit by reflected magenta is very difficult to correct without making everyone else in the frame look wrong. This is the single most common request we make of a wedding party, and it is worth passing on to anyone standing in the front row.

"Hold the trousers up to a window in the shop. If you can see your hand through them, everyone at golden hour will see more than that."

Shoes on Sand: Barefoot, Espadrilles or Leather

Sand defeats formal shoes. A leather sole has no grip on soft sand, so you slide with every step, and the walk down an aisle becomes visibly effortful in a way that shows in photographs. Salt water and fine sand are also hard on good leather, and if there is any chance of a portrait at the waterline, that is a real cost.

The honest ranking for a ceremony on sand is barefoot first, espadrilles or a soft unlined loafer second, leather third. Barefoot suits the setting, photographs beautifully, and is what many resorts effectively require anyway once you step off the aisle runner. Espadrilles or woven loafers worn without socks give you a shoe that reads intentional rather than improvised. If the ceremony is on a deck, terrace, pier or lawn instead of sand, leather is back on the table and looks excellent.

The practical answer most grooms land on is two pairs. Barefoot or espadrilles for the ceremony and beach portraits, leather for the reception once you are on a hard surface, with the change happening during cocktail hour. One warning about timing: sand at one in the afternoon is genuinely hot enough to hurt, and it is comfortable again by six. Since we plan every wedding around golden hour, this rarely comes up, but if there is a midday first look on the beach, keep something on your feet.

Coordinating Groomsmen Without Matching Them

Six men in identical rented suits standing in a line is the most common groomsmen photograph in the world and the least flattering. It reads as uniform, it reads as rental, and because rented suits are usually synthetic it also reads as shiny. Coordination beats matching every time.

The method that works is a constraint, not a costume. Give the group three rules: one fabric weight, one narrow palette, one consistent trouser tone. Then let the details vary. Shirts can shift between white, cream and a soft tone. Textures can differ. Some can wear a jacket and some can go without, and it will still look like a single group in the frame. What holds a photograph together is tonal consistency, not identical garments, and slight variation is exactly what makes editorial imagery look editorial.

Then separate yourself with one element. A jacket in a different tone, a lighter suit against a group in a deeper one, or the only tie in a group without ties. One point of difference is enough. Two or three and you look like a different wedding.

One logistics note specific to destination weddings. Do not have groomsmen suits shipped to the resort. Everyone carries their own outfit in their own carry-on, and everyone tries it on at home two weeks before, not in a hotel room the night before, when there is no tailor within an hour and no time to fix a sleeve.

Grooming, Sweat and the 30 Minutes Before the Ceremony

The last half hour decides how you look in the first twenty photographs of the ceremony, so it is worth a plan.

Apply antiperspirant the night before as well as in the morning. It works best when it goes on to cool, dry skin and has hours to take effect, which is exactly the opposite of applying it while already warm and rushing. Stay in air conditioning until we call you, not out on the beach greeting arriving guests for forty minutes. Carry blotting papers and a small hand towel, and if you shine easily, a little translucent powder is not vanity, it is the difference between a portrait and a highlight on your forehead. Pack a second identical shirt for after dinner, which costs almost nothing and rescues the reception photographs.

Two things people get wrong. Hair product that is fine in a dry climate can slide in Caribbean humidity, so choose a matte clay or paste rather than a heavy gel, and skip the wet look entirely. And go easy with facial sunscreen right before portraits. Some mineral formulas with high zinc content leave a pale cast that flash picks up, so you look grey in the frames from the reception while looking perfectly normal to everyone in the room. Use it generously all day, then wipe the face down and reapply lightly before we start.

Last one, and it is not about clothing. Eat something and drink water through the afternoon. Heat, nerves, an empty stomach and a couple of celebratory drinks during getting-ready is a combination we have watched go wrong more than once, and no fabric choice fixes it.

What to Bring for the Day-After Session

A lot of couples on this coast add a day-after or sunrise session, and it is the one place where the wedding day rules relax completely. Bring something lighter than the wedding outfit: an open linen shirt with the sleeves rolled, tailored shorts or light trousers, no jacket, no tie. Barefoot throughout.

If there is any chance of going into the water, and at sunrise on a calm morning there usually is, bring clothes you are willing to soak. Wet linen is heavy and turns translucent, so bring a second set to change into rather than travelling back through the lobby dripping. Sunrise on this coast is quiet, the light is soft for a good hour, the beach is empty, and it is genuinely the best hour of the whole trip for photographs.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should a groom wear to a beach wedding in Mexico?

A light warm neutral in a breathable natural fabric: a linen or linen-cotton suit in sand, oatmeal, stone or soft tan, worn without a waistcoat, with the jacket treated as optional after the ceremony. Skip heavy worsted wool and anything synthetic. Ivory and pale beige photograph beautifully against turquoise water and reflect heat rather than absorbing it.

Is linen too wrinkly for a wedding?

Pure linen creases by design and will look lived in by the reception, which some grooms love. If you want the look with more discipline, choose a linen-cotton or linen-wool blend, which holds a line far better through a seated dinner. Whichever you choose, hang it in an air-conditioned room the night before and put the jacket on last.

Should the groom wear shoes on the sand?

Barefoot or espadrilles for a ceremony on sand, leather for a deck or terrace reception. Leather soles slide on soft sand, salt water ruins them, and many resorts ask everyone to step off the aisle runner anyway. Bring both pairs and change between the ceremony and dinner.

Which colours photograph best against the Caribbean?

Warm neutrals win: sand, oatmeal, bone, stone, tan and camel sit beautifully next to turquoise water without competing with it. Muted sage, dusty blue and terracotta also work. Avoid saturated neons, which bounce coloured light onto faces, and be careful with pure black, which absorbs heat and flattens into a shape in strong backlight.

Do groomsmen have to match the groom exactly?

No, and identical rented suits in a row are the least flattering option on camera. Give the group one fabric weight and one narrow palette, keep the trouser tone consistent, and let shirts and textures vary slightly. Then separate the groom with one element, a different jacket, a lighter tone or the only tie in the group.

How do I stop sweating through my shirt before the ceremony?

Apply antiperspirant the night before as well as in the morning, since it works while you are still and cool. Stay in air conditioning until we call you, carry blotting papers and a small towel, and pack a second identical shirt for the reception. Also go easy on facial sunscreen right before portraits, because some mineral formulas leave a pale cast under flash.
