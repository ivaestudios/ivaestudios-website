In short

Puerto Morelos, just 20 to 25 minutes from Cancún airport, hides reef-calmed water flat enough for toddlers plus the leaning lighthouse by the pier. Akumal's turtle bay has been a regulated, protected refuge since 2016, so sessions there work around its rules; the two towns sit about 40 to 45 minutes apart on Highway 307.

Two places on this coast get overlooked by almost every visitor and both of them are better for young families than the resorts people actually book. Puerto Morelos sits twenty minutes south of Cancún airport behind a reef that flattens the sea into something closer to a pool than an ocean. Akumal, another forty minutes down the highway, has coves, sea turtles and a set of rules that most people find out about only when a guard stops them at the water. Neither town is a secret, both are genuinely worth a session, and both reward you enormously for arriving at the right hour. This is our working guide to photographing families in both.

Puerto Morelos: the reef, the calm water and the pier

Puerto Morelos is a small fishing town that never became a resort strip: a square with low buildings around it, a pier, a few restaurants, and a beach running north and south from there. It is the closest genuinely charming town to Cancún airport, and most people drive straight past the exit.

The defining feature is the reef. The Parque Nacional Arrecife de Puerto Morelos runs roughly half a kilometre offshore here and takes the energy out of the sea before it reaches the sand. What is left is shallow, clear and almost still, in a way the open coast further south is not. On a normal morning you can stand fifty metres out at waist depth with no swell at all.

The town's landmark is the leaning lighthouse, el faro inclinado, which was knocked off vertical by Hurricane Beulah in 1967 and left standing at an angle by the pier. It is a genuinely useful photographic anchor because it is instantly identifiable and it places your family in a specific town rather than on a generic beach. The pier next to it, the fishing boats moored off it and the low painted buildings around the square all photograph well and all sit within a three-minute walk of each other, which matters more than it sounds when you have children.

Orientation: the beach faces east, so this is a sunrise location. First light comes off the water directly behind you, the boats are silhouetted, and the town has not woken up. By nine the square is busy and the light is hard. The last hour of the day is soft and workable on the sand but the sun is behind the town, so there is no sunset over water here either.

Why calm water changes what is possible with toddlers

This is the practical reason we push families with small children toward Puerto Morelos, and it is worth spelling out because it is not obvious until you have watched it fail somewhere else.

On an open-coast beach with even a modest shore break, a session with a two-year-old goes one of two ways. Either the child will not go near the water, or the child loves it and a parent has one hand on them at all times while bracing against the waves. Both produce photographs of a family managing a situation rather than enjoying one.

In water that is genuinely flat, everything relaxes. A toddler can sit down in ten centimetres of water and splash without anyone tensing. Parents let go. Grandparents walk to the waterline without worrying about their footing, which is the single most common access problem in multigenerational groups. We can put six people ankle deep with the camera low and nobody is bracing.

There is a second, less obvious benefit. Still water reflects, so a family in the shallows appears twice and the light bounces up into faces from below, filling shadows under eyes and chins without any equipment. It only happens where the water does not move.

Akumal cove: turtles, rules and the honest crowd situation

Akumal means place of the turtles in Maya and it earns the name. Green sea turtles graze the seagrass beds in the bay year round, close enough to shore that you can swim out to them. That is why people come, and why the rules exist.

Since 2016 Akumal bay has been managed as a protected refuge, and the turtle grazing area is a regulated zone rather than an open beach. In practice that means a marked swimming area, guides required for the snorkel zone, mandatory life vests, a cap on how many people are in the water at once, and no sunscreen at all in the water. The rules are enforced by guards on the beach and they change in detail from season to season. The beach itself has legitimate public access on foot through the arch at the town entrance, and you should expect to be approached by people selling tours on the way in. Walking to the public beach is free.

Now the honest part about photography here. Akumal bay at sunrise is one of the prettiest curves of shoreline in the region: a wide protected cove, calm water, palms right down to the sand. By nine in the morning there are frequently several hundred people in orange life vests in that same water, plus boats, plus guides, and it is finished as a photographic location for the rest of the day. The window is real but it is narrow, and anyone who tells you otherwise has not been there at ten.

Two nearby alternatives are worth knowing. Half Moon Bay, immediately north, is quieter, rockier and lined with villas rather than tour operators, and it works at hours when the main bay does not. Yal-Ku lagoon at the far north end, where fresh and salt water mix, is a different landscape entirely and photographs beautifully, with the caveat that it is an entry-fee attraction with its own opening hours and its own snorkelling crowd.

Akumal in one sentence

Be on the sand before seven and Akumal is one of the best family locations on this coast. Arrive at nine and you are photographing a queue. There is no version of this where you show up late and it works out.

Drive times and airport proximity

Puerto Morelos is the closest good beach town to Cancún International Airport: 20 to 25 minutes from the terminals, faster than reaching most of the Hotel Zone. From the Hotel Zone itself, 35 to 45 minutes. From Playa del Carmen, about 25 minutes north.

Akumal is further. Plan 55 to 70 minutes from Cancún airport, about 30 minutes south of Playa del Carmen, and about 25 minutes north of Tulum. Puerto Morelos to Akumal is roughly 40 to 45 minutes down Highway 307.

That proximity is why we recommend Puerto Morelos to a specific kind of client: families arriving late in the day, families leaving on an afternoon flight who want one last morning, and anyone on a short trip who does not want to spend three hours of it in a van. A sunrise session here and a midday flight out of Cancún is a comfortable combination.

One caution on Highway 307: roadworks and Tren Maya construction have changed access points and lane layouts without notice for several years, so plan for the drive taking longer than the map says.

Sargassum exposure on this stretch versus Tulum

Both towns sit on the east-facing Caribbean coast, so both get sargassum, and anyone promising you a clear beach is not being straight with you. The exposure is not identical along the coast, though. Tulum's beach road takes some of the heaviest and most consistent sargassum on this coastline, because it is open, exposed and directly in the path of the currents that carry the weed in. Akumal, in a protected cove, tends to accumulate rather than be scoured, which means when a load arrives it can sit in the bay. Puerto Morelos has the reef, which reduces wave energy but does not stop sargassum, and in a heavy week the weed collects between the reef and the shore.

Over several seasons we have found this middle stretch generally has better weeks than Tulum and worse weeks than the sheltered north side around Costa Mujeres. Heavy season runs roughly April through August, worst in May, June and July, and it swings week to week with the wind. Our sargassum season guide has the month by month picture.

What we actually do about it. We check the shoreline the morning of your session, not the week before, because it changes in two days. If the sand is heavy, we photograph from the sand looking inland, use the town square and the pier in Puerto Morelos, use the palms and the villa lanes at Half Moon Bay, and we keep the waterline out of the frame. A cenote is the other honest answer, and there are excellent ones within twenty minutes of both towns. We cover that in our cenote photography page.

"The reef does not stop the seaweed. It stops the waves. Those are two different gifts and only one of them is what people think they are buying."

Where to eat and rest between setups

This is a real logistical question with children and it decides whether the last twenty minutes of a session are any good.

In Puerto Morelos everything is on the square, which is the town's great advantage. It sits one minute from the beach, has shade, benches and low walls, and the cafés and bakeries around it open early, with a fishermen's cooperative by the pier serving ceviche later on. You can pause mid-session, get everyone a drink, let a toddler have a real break and go back out without leaving a hundred metre radius.

Akumal is more limited at sunrise. The small plaza inside the entrance arch has a couple of early places and Half Moon Bay has small restaurants along the road behind it, but most open later. For a dawn session there, bring water and snacks and plan breakfast for afterwards.

A note that applies to both. Bring a small bag with water, wipes, a towel, a change of clothes for anyone under six, and something to eat. The most common reason a session ends early is a hungry child.

A half-day plan for a family of six

Here is a plan we have run many times, for two parents, two grandparents and two children under eight, based in or near Puerto Morelos.

Twenty minutes before sunrise, the beach north of the pier. Empty sand, flat water, soft blue light. We start with the whole group of six while everyone is fresh, because a full group photograph is the hardest one to get and should never be left until the end.

Sunrise, the waterline. Everyone ankle deep, children in the water, grandparents on the firm wet sand where the footing is good. This is the twenty minutes that produces most of the frames people print.

Sunrise plus twenty-five, pairs and individuals. Grandparents alone, then each nuclear family, then the parents, then each child, so whoever is finished can go and sit down. Then a ten-minute break on the square at around forty-five minutes: water, a pastry, shade. That break is not lost time, it resets the children completely.

Sunrise plus sixty, the pier and the leaning lighthouse. Fifteen minutes with the boats and the landmark, which is what makes the gallery look like Puerto Morelos rather than anywhere.

Sunrise plus eighty, done. Breakfast, the whole day still ahead, and the gallery within one to three business days.

If Akumal is where you are staying, the same structure works with the times pulled fifteen minutes earlier and the break removed, because the crowd arrives sooner. The studio is based in Cancún and photographs this whole stretch of the Riviera Maya every week. Questions families ask about Puerto Morelos and Akumal

Is Puerto Morelos better than Cancun for a family with toddlers?

For water safety and comfort, yes. The reef half a kilometre offshore takes the energy out of the sea, so the water at Puerto Morelos is shallow and close to still, while the Cancun Hotel Zone's open Caribbean side often has a real shore break. Flat water means a toddler can sit and splash without a parent bracing, and grandparents can reach the waterline safely.

How far is Puerto Morelos from Cancun airport?

About 20 to 25 minutes, which makes it the closest genuinely attractive beach town to the terminals, closer in time than most of the Cancun Hotel Zone. That proximity is why we recommend it for families on short trips, for a first morning after a late arrival, or for one last session before an afternoon flight home.

What are the rules at Akumal bay?

Akumal bay has been a protected refuge since 2016 and the turtle grazing area is regulated. Expect a marked swimming zone, guides required for snorkelling, mandatory life vests, a limit on how many people are in the water, and no sunscreen in the water at all. Guards enforce this on the beach and the details change by season. Public access on foot through the town arch is free.

What time should we photograph at Akumal?

Before seven in the morning. Akumal bay at first light is one of the loveliest coves on the coast, calm and empty, but by nine there are often several hundred people in life vests in the same water along with the boats and guides. The window is genuine and it is short. Half Moon Bay just north stays workable at hours when the main bay does not.

Does the reef at Puerto Morelos stop sargassum?

No. The reef reduces wave energy, which is why the water is calm, but it does not block the weed, and in a heavy week sargassum can collect between the reef and the shore. Heavy season on this coast runs roughly April through August. We check the shoreline on the morning of your session and shift to the town square, the pier or a nearby cenote if the sand is bad.

Can you photograph a group of six including grandparents here?

Yes, and Puerto Morelos is one of the easier places for it. The firm wet sand near the waterline gives good footing, the water is flat enough that nobody is bracing against waves, and the beach, the square and the pier all sit within a few minutes' walk so nobody has to keep moving. We photograph the full group first while everyone is fresh, then work down to individuals.
