In short

Traveling alone with the kids usually means you are the one holding the phone, so you are missing from the photos. A session puts you back in them: all of you together, you with each child, and the kids on their own. If you are the only adult on this trip, you are also the photographer. Scroll through your camera roll after a week in Cancun and you will find the kids in the pool, the kids on the sand, the kids asleep on the drive back, and almost nothing of you with them. A single parent family photoshoot is made for exactly that gap: for 45 minutes or an hour, someone else holds the camera and you get to be in the picture, holding the hand you usually photograph.

This guide is for single moms and dads, and for any parent flying down with the kids while the other stays home.

The Photos a Solo Parent Never Gets

When two adults travel together, each one usually shows up in some of the photos the other takes. When you travel alone, the person missing from every frame is you. These are the mom and kids, or dad and kids, photos to ask for:

  • All of you together. The one for the wall, taken first, while clothes are clean and everyone is fresh.
  • You with each child, one at a time. Give each pairing its own moment: a shoulder ride with your youngest, like the one at the top of this page, then a slow walk along the water with your oldest.
  • The kids together. Siblings sitting close on the sand or racing the foam line, with nobody behind a phone telling them to look up.
  • Each child alone. A child looking at you, standing just behind us, rather than at the lens, often gives the softest face.
  • You, on your own. One or two frames, if you want them. If you are always the parent behind the camera, you may not have a recent photo of yourself either.

If it is just you and one child, a two-person session works differently, and we cover it in our guides to father and son photos and mother and daughter photos.

How It Works With No Second Adult

In a two-parent session, the other adult holds the toddler, fixes a collar and fetches whoever wandered off. When you are the only adult, there is nobody to hand things to.

You are in the frame; we do the directing

Because you are in the photos, you cannot also be the one running them. That part is ours: where to stand, when to walk, how to win a laugh from your youngest. You hold hands and play. A parent of two wrote:

“She knew exactly how to keep them engaged and smiling naturally, which is no small task on a sandy beach in the sun!”

Danielle P., Google review

An order that works with one adult

  1. All of you together, first, while energy is high.
  2. You with your youngest, before their good minutes run out.
  3. You with each of the others, one at a time.
  4. The kids together, then each child alone.
  5. A last walk, all of you heading down the beach.

If a mood dips, we change the order; a snack or a few minutes of sandcastle building often resets things.

The other kids stay close

While you are photographed with one child, the others need somewhere to be. Keep it simple: a bucket, a sandcastle or a shell hunt a few steps away, where you can see them. They may wander into the frame, and that is fine.

If a grandparent, an aunt or a friend is on the trip, bring them. There is no per-person charge, so they can join a few frames and keep an eye on the others between yours. And if the trip is grandparents with the grandkids and no parents at all, see our guide to skip-generation trips.

Nobody to fix hair between frames

Dress the kids last, right before you leave the room, in clothes they have worn before. Pack one small pouch: hair ties, a comb, wipes, water and a snack. If something needs fixing mid-session, say so; a short pause costs nothing.

Stay close to your room

A session on the beach at your own resort means no taxi and no car seats to wrangle with several kids in tow. Some hotels charge their own fee for outside photographers, paid by you to the hotel; we confirm your hotel's rule before the session. As one reviewer put it:

“They met us on the beach right by our resort and led us through a great session”

Phil L., Google review

Which Collection for One Adult and Two or Three Kids

Our prices depend on the collection, not the headcount: there is no per-person charge and no limit on people. For a solo parent with two or three children, two collections fit:

  • The Keepsake: 45 minutes, 25 edited photos, 1 look, 375 USD.
  • The Chapter: 60 minutes, 35 edited photos, 1 look, 475 USD, the collection we recommend for families.

Choose The Keepsake with two kids who are happy in front of a camera: 45 minutes covers all of you together, you with each child and the kids together. Choose The Chapter with three kids, with a toddler who needs breaks, or if you want a few frames of yourself: it adds 15 minutes and 10 photos.

The Postcard, our 35-minute collection, is made for couples. And if you book The Keepsake and decide you want The Chapter, you move up by paying only the difference: 100 USD.

Traveling Alone With Kids: What Official Sources Say

We are photographers, not immigration advisers, so here is only what official sources said when we read them on November 14, 2026. Rules change: check them, and your airline, before you fly.

  • US Department of State, Mexico travel advisory (country information last updated August 11, 2025, read in its data feed): travelers under 18 who are not traveling with both parents may need a notarized parental consent letter, and it advises bringing one from any parent who is not on the trip.
  • Government of Canada, consent letter for children (modified September 2, 2026): it recommends one when a child travels with only one parent. It is not legally required in Canada, but immigration authorities abroad, airline agents and Canadian officials may ask for it; Canada strongly recommends a notary public as witness and says original signed letters are best.
  • Mexico's National Migration Institute (INM), exit form for minors, the SAM (page dated July 30, 2026, in Spanish): the form is not required when a child travels with one of their parents.

Passports and the rest of the entry rules are in our guides for US travelers and Canadian travelers.

The Keepsake is 45 minutes and 25 edited photos for 375 USD. The Chapter, our recommended family collection, is 60 minutes and 35 photos for 475 USD. With three kids, a toddler who needs breaks or a wish for photos of yourself, The Chapter leaves more room.
