If Your Child Gets Sick in Cancun: Doctors, Hospitals and Pharmacies

In short

For an emergency, dial 911. For a fever or an earache, call your hotel's front desk, then a hospital: the US Embassy's list names four in Cancun. Expect to pay up front and claim later. Give children bottled water, pack their medicines and use repellent, above all at sunrise and sunset: Quintana Roo had 623 confirmed dengue cases in 2026 by November 9.

A fever on day three, an earache after the pool, a stomach bug before the flight home. We are photographers, not doctors, so this guide sticks to official and primary sources, all checked on November 14, 2026, plus what to do around your photo session.

Who to Call First: Hotel Doctor, Hospital or 911

In an emergency, call 911 for an ambulance, the number the US Embassy gives for Mexico. Canada notes that most healthcare providers there don't speak English or French, so ask a bilingual hotel employee to help with the call.

Otherwise, start with your hotel's front desk: ask whether there is a doctor on site or on call, what a visit costs and whether the doctor bills insurers. For a hospital, the US Embassy's list for the Merida consular district, which covers Cancun, names four in the city: Hospital Amerimed Cancun, Hospital Azura, Hospital Galenia and the public Hospital General de Cancun. On their own websites, Amerimed lists pediatrics and a 24/7 emergency room with bilingual specialists, and Galenia a 24-hour emergency service and a pediatric intensive care unit. At the hospital, ask for a pediatrician: pediatra, in Spanish. In Playa del Carmen, the list names Amerimed, Costamed and Hospiten. Being listed is not an endorsement, the State Department notes.

Paying Up Front, Claiming Later

Hospitals in Mexico usually require payment up front and place holds on credit cards at admission, the US Embassy says, and Canada adds that most private facilities don't deal directly with insurers. Amerimed's insurance page says that where it has no agreement with your insurer, you claim the reimbursement yourself. Medicare does not cover care outside the United States, and Canada says most provincial and territorial plans are extremely limited abroad.

By Mexican law, the embassy explains, the nearest facility must see an emergency patient, and a hospital may not hold a patient or a passport to guarantee payment. For the claim:

  • Call your insurer's assistance line before paying, if you can. The CDC suggests a policy that pays hospitals directly.
  • Ask for a written estimate before treatment and an itemized bill every day, as the embassy recommends.
  • Keep every receipt, the doctor's report and each prescription.

Pharmacies in Cancun: What to Buy, What to Skip

Canada's advice is direct: in Mexico, many medicines, over the counter and prescription, are readily available with little oversight, and counterfeit medication is common in certain parts of the country, so get a local prescription and buy from a reputable pharmacy. The CDC adds: use licensed pharmacies, ask for a receipt, show the pharmacist the medicine you use at home and check the packaging. Outside the US, acetaminophen is called paracetamol.

The CDC is specific:

  • Diarrhea in children: plenty of fluids, with oral rehydration salts to prevent dehydration; usually there is no need for medicine.
  • No bismuth products, such as Pepto-Bismol or Kaopectate, for children, and no loperamide under age 6.
  • Itchy bites: hydrocortisone cream or calamine lotion, and no scratching.

Simplest of all, pack your children's usual medicines in their original containers with copies of prescriptions, as Canada advises, and a card in Spanish listing allergies and the generic names of their medicines, as the CDC suggests. Ask your pediatrician about doses, and about the CDC's advice for Mexico: an early MMR dose and a hepatitis A dose for babies 6 to 11 months. More in our packing list for Cancun with kids.

Can Kids Drink the Water in Cancun? And When to Worry

Give children bottled or purified water, and use it to brush teeth and mix formula: the CDC's advice wherever tap water might be contaminated. Canada's advice for Mexico is to drink water from a safe source and avoid food washed in local drinking water. Ask your hotel whether the water in your room and the ice are purified.

Food matters too. The CDC reports increased cases of cyclosporiasis, an illness caused by a parasite in food or water, in travelers returning from Mexico, including Cancun and the Riviera Maya. Canada notes that young children are more at risk of travelers' diarrhea and that the most important thing is to keep drinking fluids. The CDC's line: if your child seems dehydrated, has a fever or has bloody stools, get medical attention immediately.

Mosquitoes and Dengue: The 2026 Numbers

In the weekly bulletins of Mexico's health ministry, Quintana Roo, the state Cancun is in, had 98 confirmed dengue cases in 2026 by June 8 and 623 by November 9, nearly three times the 229 at the same point of 2025. Canada says the mosquitoes that carry dengue typically bite during the daytime, particularly around sunrise and sunset, and the CDC lists dengue among the diseases in Mexico that cannot be prevented with a vaccine. Avoiding bites is the defense, and it also helps against chikungunya and Zika.

  • Under 6 months: no DEET or icaridin; a mosquito net over the crib or stroller instead (Health Canada).
  • 6 months to 12 years: DEET up to 10%, at most once a day under age 2 and up to three times a day from 2 to 12 (Health Canada). No oil of lemon eucalyptus or PMD under 3 (CDC).
  • Sunscreen first, repellent second. Spray repellent on your hands, then apply it to the child's face, never to their hands (CDC).

If a child runs a fever, see a doctor. For dengue, the CDC advises rest, fluids and acetaminophen, not aspirin or ibuprofen, and an emergency room at once for warning signs such as belly pain, vomiting at least three times in 24 hours, bleeding from the nose or gums, or a child who seems tired, restless or irritable. They usually begin 24 to 48 hours after the fever has gone away.

Is Sargassum Harmful? About That Smell

The brown seaweed itself is not toxic, and people can swim around it, according to the University of South Florida's Sargassum Watch. Under Caribbean sun, though, beached sargassum can decompose in one to two days and release hydrogen sulfide and ammonia, which mainly affect people with respiratory problems such as asthma. Hydrogen sulfide is the rotten-egg smell; the US toxic substances agency, ATSDR, says it is not known whether children are more sensitive to it than adults.

So keep children, above all any with asthma, away from large rotting piles; small amounts, the lab says, can simply be ignored. Amounts change by month and by beach: see Cancun's seaweed season, month by month.
