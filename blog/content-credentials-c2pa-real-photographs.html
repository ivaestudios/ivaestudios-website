In short

They prove a file's chain of custody, not that the scene was real, and a missing credential proves nothing. EU AI Act transparency rules begin applying 2 August 2026.

Nobody has asked us yet whether a photograph in their gallery is real. That question is coming, and the infrastructure to answer it is already shipping. Content Credentials, the provenance standard built by the C2PA, now appear in cameras from Leica, Nikon, Canon, Sony and Fujifilm and in Google's Pixel phones. Getty has reported that roughly nine in ten consumers want to know whether an image was made with artificial intelligence. And from 2 August 2026 the European Union's AI Act begins applying transparency obligations to synthetic media. For a studio whose entire product is a truthful photograph of something that actually happened, this is worth understanding before a client raises it. Here is what the standard does, what it genuinely proves, where it falls short today, and what we do in the meantime.

What C2PA Is, in Plain Language

C2PA stands for the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity, a standards body formed by Adobe, Microsoft, the BBC, Intel, Arm, Truepic and others. The thing it produces is a technical specification. The thing you see as a user is called Content Credentials, and it is best understood as a nutrition label attached to a file.

When a supporting camera makes a photograph, it writes a small signed record into the file: what device made it, when, and a cryptographic signature tied to a certificate. When the file is later opened in supporting software, each meaningful edit can be appended to that record. Crop, colour grade, generative fill, all of it can be listed.

Two properties make it more than metadata. It is cryptographically signed, so tampering with the record breaks the signature and shows up as invalid rather than passing silently. And it is a chain rather than a single stamp, so the history travels with the file instead of being replaced at each step.

What it is not is a watermark, and it is not a detector. C2PA never examines the pixels to guess whether something is synthetic. It only reports what the tools that touched the file chose to declare. That distinction is the whole subject of the next few sections.

Which Cameras and Phones Sign Images Today

Leica went first, shipping the M11-P in 2023 as the first production camera with Content Credentials written at capture. Since then support has arrived across the professional market: Nikon has enabled it on bodies including the Z6III and Z8, Canon has brought it to the EOS R5 Mark II and R1, Sony has rolled it out by firmware across its professional line, and Fujifilm has added it to the GFX series. Most of these arrived as firmware updates rather than new hardware, which is why the list moves faster than any article can track.

On the phone side, Google has built Content Credentials into recent Pixel devices and Photos, which matters enormously for scale. Phones make most of the world's photographs, and a standard confined to twelve thousand dollar camera bodies would stay a curiosity.

The editing side is further along than the capture side. Adobe's Photoshop and Lightroom can attach and preserve Content Credentials, and generative tools from several vendors now mark their output. In practice a photograph today is far more likely to carry a record of how it was edited than a record of how it was captured, and honestly, that is the more useful half.

Two caveats before anyone gets excited. Support is per model and often per firmware version, so owning a supported brand is not the same as owning a supported body. And on most cameras it is a setting that must be switched on rather than a default.

What a credential does not tell you

That the scene was real. A signed camera pointed at a screen produces a perfectly valid credential for a photograph of a fake. Provenance proves the chain of custody of a file, not the truthfulness of what was in front of the lens. Anyone selling it as proof of reality is overselling it.

What Credentials Prove, and the Missing Timestamp Problem

A valid credential tells you that a file was signed by a particular device or piece of software, and that it has not been altered since, and it lists whatever edits the chain declared. That is genuinely useful. It is also narrower than most people assume.

The largest gap in practice is absence. If a photograph carries no credential, that means nothing at all. It might be a synthetic image, or it might be a perfectly ordinary photograph from a camera that does not support the standard, or a real photograph that lost its record when a platform stripped the metadata on upload. Most social platforms still do exactly that. So credentials can support a claim of authenticity but their absence can never support a claim of fakery, and any system that treats missing credentials as suspicious will punish honest photographers with older equipment.

Then there is the timestamp issue, which is more technical and worth knowing about because it affects wedding photographs specifically. Signatures rely on certificates, and certificates expire. Without a trusted, independently verifiable timestamp recording that the signature was made while its certificate was still valid, a signature can become unverifiable years later even though nothing was ever tampered with. The specification has been extended to incorporate trusted time stamping to address this, but coverage across shipping implementations is uneven and older signed files may not carry it.

For a news photograph, verification happens within days and this rarely matters. For a wedding album that a family opens in 2050, it matters a great deal, and it is the single reason we do not yet treat credentials as an archival guarantee.

The EU Rules That Apply From August 2026

The European Union's AI Act phases in over several years, and 2 August 2026 is the date on which its transparency obligations for synthetic content start applying. In broad terms, providers of systems that generate synthetic image, audio or video content must mark that output in a machine readable way, and deployers who publish synthetic content that could mislead have disclosure duties of their own.

The Act does not name C2PA. It sets an outcome, machine readable marking, and leaves the mechanism open, which is exactly why C2PA has become the default answer: it already exists, it is open, and it is implemented across the major vendors.

Read plainly, this is regulation aimed at generators rather than at photographers. A studio delivering photographs of a real wedding is not producing synthetic content and has no obligation under it. What changes is the environment. Once European platforms are labelling synthetic images at scale, the absence of a label starts being read as a signal, and clients who see labels everywhere else will eventually ask about the photographs they paid for. We would rather have an answer ready than improvise one.

Why a Luxury Studio Should Care Before Clients Ask

Three reasons, in ascending order of how much they matter to us.

The commercial one is straightforward. If most consumers want to know whether an image is synthetic, then being able to demonstrate that yours are not is a differentiator that costs nothing to be honest about.

The professional one concerns the middle ground. Almost every modern retouching tool now includes generative features, and the line between removing a stray hair and replacing a sky is not obvious to a client looking at a finished frame. A provenance record makes that distinction explicit rather than a matter of trust. We think the industry will end up needing that clarity more than it currently believes.

The one we actually care about is what we sell. IVAE was founded in 2023 on a very narrow proposition: golden hour only, real light, real places, delivered fast. The reason we shoot at sunrise and in the last forty minutes before sunset instead of fixing bad midday light afterwards is that we would rather the photograph be true than convenient. Provenance is simply the technical expression of that position, and if a standard arrives that lets us prove it rather than assert it, we want to be early to it.

How to Check Whether an Image Carries Credentials

The public verification tool lives at contentcredentials.org/verify. Upload or drag in an image and it will report whether a manifest is present, what it says, and whether the signature validates. Some viewing software shows a small credential icon in the corner of an image when a manifest is present, and clicking it opens the same information.

Three practical notes if you try it. Test the original file rather than one you saved from a website, because most platforms strip metadata on upload and you will usually get a false negative. Screenshots always lose everything, since a screenshot is a new file made by a different program. And a report of no credentials found is not evidence of anything, for the reasons above.

If you receive photographs from us and want to check something, download the original from the gallery rather than saving a preview, and ask us. We keep the RAW originals and can answer any question about how a specific frame was made.

What We Do Today While the Standard Matures

Honest answer first: our capture chain does not sign every file at the moment, because support is per body and per firmware and we shoot on gear chosen for how it renders skin in tropical light rather than for its provenance features. Claiming otherwise would be exactly the kind of overstatement this article is arguing against.

What we do instead is keep the substance of the claim. We retain the RAW originals, which are the real provenance record: a RAW file is the camera's own negative and it is the strongest evidence that a frame was captured rather than generated. We deliver photographs, not composites, so what you receive is the moment that happened, with colour, exposure and retouching applied to it and no elements added that were not there. If a client ever wants a composite, a group photograph assembled from two frames or a sky replaced, we will do it and we will say so in writing. And we plan to enable capture side Content Credentials as the bodies we shoot support them properly, including trusted timestamps, because a partial implementation that fails verification in twenty years is worse than none.

Common Questions About This

What are Content Credentials?

Content Credentials are a signed record attached to an image file describing what device or software made it and what edits were applied afterwards. They are produced under the C2PA specification, a standard developed by a coalition including Adobe, Microsoft, the BBC, Intel, Arm and Truepic. The record is cryptographically signed, so altering it invalidates the signature rather than passing unnoticed, and it accumulates as a chain rather than being replaced at each step.

Which cameras support C2PA?

Leica shipped the first production camera with Content Credentials in the M11-P in 2023, and support has since reached bodies including the Nikon Z6III and Z8, the Canon EOS R5 Mark II and R1, Sony professional bodies by firmware and the Fujifilm GFX series, alongside recent Google Pixel phones. Most of that arrived as firmware rather than new hardware, support is per model and per firmware version, and on most cameras it has to be switched on rather than being a default.

Does a missing credential mean a photo is fake?

No, and this is the most important misunderstanding about the standard. An image can lack a credential because it came from a camera that does not support C2PA, because it was edited in software that does not write one, or because a social platform stripped the metadata when it was uploaded, which most platforms still do. Credentials can support a claim of authenticity, but their absence can never support a claim of fakery.

Can Content Credentials prove a photograph is real?

They prove custody, not truth. A credential shows that a file was signed by a given device or program and has not been altered since, and it lists whatever edits the chain declared. A signed camera pointed at a screen would still produce a valid credential for a photograph of a fake image. Provenance answers where a file came from, not whether the scene in front of the lens was genuine.

What is the timestamp problem with C2PA?

Signatures depend on certificates, and certificates expire. Without a trusted, independently verifiable timestamp proving the signature was made while its certificate was still valid, a signature can become unverifiable years later even though nothing was tampered with. The specification has been extended to add trusted time stamping, but coverage across shipping implementations is uneven and older signed files may lack it. For a news photograph verified within days this rarely matters. For a wedding album opened in fifty years it matters a great deal.

Do the EU AI Act rules affect my wedding photographs?

Not directly. The transparency obligations that begin applying on 2 August 2026 are aimed at systems that generate synthetic image, audio or video content and at people who publish such content in ways that could mislead. A studio delivering photographs of a real wedding is not producing synthetic content. What changes is the environment: once labels are common, their absence starts being read as a signal, which is why we would rather have a clear answer ready than improvise one later.

The short version: Content Credentials are real, useful and shipping, they prove custody rather than truth, absence of a credential proves nothing, and the timestamp gap means today's signatures are not yet an archival guarantee. For a wedding studio the standard is not a threat, it is a vocabulary for something we were already doing. The photographs are of things that happened, at the hour we said we would photograph them, and we will keep being able to prove that whichever way the technology settles.
