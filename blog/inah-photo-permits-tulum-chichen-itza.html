In short

Professional photos inside the Tulum ruins or Chichen Itza need a discretionary INAH permit: recent federal fee schedules put still photography at roughly MXN $7,958 per day, filed about ten business days ahead, with video charged separately. The zones also close before golden hour, so most couples shoot limestone-and-jungle locations outside the sites instead.

Every few weeks someone asks us the same question, usually two months before the wedding and usually with a Pinterest board attached: can we do the photos at the Tulum ruins? Or at Chichen Itza, with the pyramid behind us? The honest answer is not the one anyone wants, so most photographers give a vague one instead. Here is the real version, including the numbers, the paperwork, the reasons behind the rules, and the places along this coast that give you the same feeling without a confrontation at a gate on your wedding morning.

Who INAH Is and What It Controls

INAH is the Instituto Nacional de Antropología e Historia, the federal body that guards Mexico's archaeological heritage. It is not a tourism office and it does not think about your wedding album. Its mandate is conservation, and every archaeological zone in the country falls under it: Tulum, Cobá, Chichen Itza, Ek Balam, Muyil, El Rey inside the Cancún Hotel Zone, San Gervasio on Cozumel, all of them. Museums and historic monuments fall under the same umbrella.

The practical consequence is that an archaeological zone is federal property with its own rules of use, not a public park and certainly not a venue. Entrance tickets buy you the right to visit, walk the marked paths and take personal photographs. They do not buy the right to hold an event, block a walkway, set up equipment or run a commercial production. That single distinction explains almost everything that follows.

The short version

Personal photos on your visit: fine, included with your ticket. A styled or professional shoot, with a hired photographer, gown, assistants or gear: requires a permit obtained in advance. A wedding ceremony, vow renewal or private event inside the zone: not authorized at all. A drone: not without federal written authorization, which these shoots do not get.

Tripods, Professional Gear and the Equipment Fee

The first thing that surprises visitors is that tripods are restricted. Not banned in a dramatic way, but routinely refused at the entrance unless the visit is covered by authorization. Monopods, stabilizers, gimbals and light stands land in the same category. The reason is partly crowd flow, since these sites move thousands of people a day along narrow roped paths, and partly monument protection, since anything with legs eventually ends up leaning on limestone that is a thousand years old.

Video equipment carries its own charge on the INAH fee schedule, separate from the still-photography permit, and this is where a lot of couples get caught out. A videographer with a rig is unmistakably professional, and the same handheld allowance that quietly lets a tourist film on a phone does not extend to a two-person crew. If you are working with a film team as well as a photographer, both have to be declared.

The Permit Process, Cost and Lead Time

Professional photography at an archaeological zone runs through a formal request to INAH that describes the site, the date, the purpose, the crew size and the equipment list. Recent federal fee schedules have put professional still photography at archaeological zones at roughly MXN $7,958 per day, with video equipment charged separately, and the guidance couples are given is to file around ten business days before the intended date.

Two caveats matter more than the number. First, these fees sit inside the Ley Federal de Derechos, which is revised every year, so the figure moves and any blog post, including this one, ages quickly. Confirm current amounts with INAH directly rather than trusting a number you read online. Second, and more importantly, a permit is discretionary. Paying the fee is not the same as being approved. Requests tied to commercial advertising, fashion, and anything that reads as an event are commonly declined, and approvals that do come through usually arrive with conditions attached: restricted hours, restricted areas, an INAH representative accompanying the crew, no blocking of visitor circulation.

For a wedding on a fixed date, that combination of ten business days, discretionary approval and conditional access is the deal breaker. You cannot build a wedding-day timeline on a maybe. It is a workable path for an editorial production planned months out with flexible dates. It is not a workable path for a bride who needs to be somewhere at 5:40 PM on a Saturday.

"A permit is not a receipt. Paying the fee gets your request read. It does not guarantee your wedding morning."

Why a Bridal Session at Chichen Itza Is Off the Table

Chichen Itza deserves its own paragraph because it is the one couples ask for by name, and because it is the hardest no on this list. It is a UNESCO World Heritage site, one of the most visited monuments in the Americas, and the crowd management is on a different scale from anywhere else in the Yucatán. El Castillo has been closed to climbing since 2006, the roped perimeters are enforced by guards who move constantly, and by ten in the morning the central plaza holds a dense crowd of visitors and vendors.

Add the practical geography. Chichen Itza is roughly two and a half to three hours from Cancún by road, and closer to two hours from Valladolid. A bride in a gown makes that drive in heat and humidity, arrives into a plaza with thousands of people and no shade, and cannot use a tripod, cannot stage anything, cannot ask anyone to move, and cannot stay past closing. Even with authorization in hand, the resulting frames are a crowded midday plaza with hard overhead sun, which is the exact light we spend our careers avoiding.

We say this plainly to every couple who asks: the version of Chichen Itza you have seen on a moodboard is either a permitted editorial production shot at dawn with an INAH escort, a composite, or a different site entirely. It is not something a wedding photographer delivers on a wedding weekend.

Drones Over Archaeological Zones

Flying a drone over an INAH zone without written federal authorization is prohibited, and the enforcement is real rather than theoretical. Security at Tulum and Chichen Itza watches for aircraft, and the standard outcome for an unauthorized flight is confiscation of the drone plus an administrative fine.

Our position is unambiguous. We do not fly over archaeological zones, and if a vendor offers to do it for you, that tells you something about how they will handle the rest of your day. Aerial coverage is beautiful, and there are large stretches of the Riviera Maya coastline, resort grounds and open beach where it is straightforward and legal. Those are the places to use it.

Five Legal Alternatives That Give the Same Look

Here is the part that matters, because in ten years of shooting this coast we have never had a couple who actually wanted a specific pyramid. What they want is stone, texture, shadow, jungle and the sense of standing somewhere ancient. All of that is available without a permit.

  • Cenotes with exposed limestone. The strongest substitute by a distance. Cenotes along the Tulum to Cobá corridor give you raw rock walls, hanging roots and a single shaft of light through the ceiling that reads as cathedral architecture. Our cenote photography in Tulum page shows what that light does around mid-morning, which is the window when the beam lands.
  • The Tulum beach road. The stretch south of the archaeological zone runs along low limestone shelf, sea grape and palm, with the same coastline the ruins overlook. It photographs like the ruins without the fence, and golden hour there is unbeatable.
  • Resort architecture built on Mayan references. Several Riviera Maya properties have stone-clad walls, water channels and stepped forms designed to echo the ancient sites. You get the geometry, the shade, a bathroom, and no closing time. Our resort and venue directory notes which properties carry that language.
  • Private haciendas inland toward Valladolid. Colonial rather than pre-Hispanic, but old stone, arcades and enormous walls, bookable as private property, with full permission to bring lights and stay after dark.
  • Jungle clearings and sacbe-style paths. The green tunnels on the roads inland from Akumal and Tulum give the canopy light people associate with the ruins, with no gate to pass and no clock running.

Cobá, Muyil and Ek Balam: Access and Restrictions

Not every archaeological zone behaves the same way, and the smaller ones are worth understanding because their rules are identical but their atmosphere is not.

Cobá sits about forty five minutes inland from Tulum, spread across several kilometres of jungle with paths connecting the groups. It is far quieter than Tulum, it opens early, and because the site is so large the visitor density on the outer paths is low. The same INAH rules apply, so no professional production without a permit and no tripods, but for a couple visiting normally and shooting handheld it is the most forgiving of the three. The Nohoch Mul pyramid has been closed to climbing since 2020.

Muyil, inside the Sian Ka'an biosphere south of Tulum, is the smallest and the most atmospheric. Dense jungle, a raised boardwalk to the lagoon, and very few visitors on a weekday morning. It is also the most fragile, and the biosphere layers its own conservation rules on top of the INAH ones. Treat it as a place to visit rather than a place to work.

Ek Balam, north of Valladolid, is roughly two hours from the coast. It still allows visitors to climb its main acropolis, which is rare in the Yucatán now, and the stucco frieze there is extraordinary. The distance makes it a full day trip rather than something you fold into a wedding weekend, so we suggest it as an add-on for couples staying on after the wedding rather than as a session location.

What we do instead

When a couple sends us a ruins moodboard, we take the images apart and name what is actually in them: warm stone, deep shade, green canopy, a horizon line. Then we build a session from real locations that carry those elements, at the hour the light cooperates. Nobody has ever asked for the pyramid back once they see the gallery.

Planning the Session Around Real Rules

One last practical note on timing, because it is where most of the disappointment lives. Archaeological zones in Quintana Roo generally open around 8 or 9 in the morning and close in the mid to late afternoon, well before golden hour. That alone rules out the light we want. Combine it with midday crowds, no support gear, restricted paths and the heat, and even a fully permitted visit hands you the worst conditions of the day.

The studio is based in Cancún and works the whole coast every week, so we know which cenote is quiet on a Tuesday, which stretch of the Tulum beach road is passable after rain, and which resorts will let a couple who is not staying there walk the grounds. If you are planning a wedding here, our destination wedding photography in Mexico page covers how we build a whole day, and the Riviera Maya wedding photographer page goes deeper on this specific stretch of coastline. We cap the studio at twelve weddings a year precisely so there is time to scout properly before your date.

Rules change. Fee schedules are revised annually, sites close areas for restoration without much notice, and enforcement varies. If a permitted archaeological shoot genuinely matters to you, start the conversation with INAH months ahead and treat the answer as uncertain until it is in writing. For everything else, this coast has more usable stone and better light than any monument will hand you between nine and four.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I have a wedding ceremony inside the Tulum ruins?
No. INAH does not authorize weddings, vow renewals or ceremonies inside the archaeological zone at Tulum. The site is a protected federal monument, it is open to the general public during visiting hours, and the authorized uses do not include private events. Studios that show ceremony photos with a ruin in frame have almost always shot from a beach or a hotel property outside the fence, or at a privately owned replica. We will tell you honestly which of the two you are looking at.
Do I need a permit to take photos at Tulum with a professional camera?
For personal snapshots with a handheld camera or a phone, no permit is needed beyond your entrance ticket. The moment the shoot becomes professional, meaning a hired photographer, styled wardrobe, assistants, lighting or anything that reads as a production, INAH requires a formal permit issued in advance. Site staff make that judgement on the spot, and a bride in a gown with a photographer beside her is never read as a tourist snapshot.
How much does an INAH photo permit cost and how long does it take?
Professional still-photography permits at archaeological zones have been published in recent fee schedules at roughly MXN $7,958 per day, with additional charges for video equipment, and applications are generally expected around ten business days before the shoot date. Those figures change with the annual Ley Federal de Derechos, and approval is discretionary rather than automatic, so treat any number you read online, including this one, as a starting point and confirm directly with INAH before you build a timeline around it.
Are tripods allowed at Tulum and Chichen Itza?
Tripods, monopods and stabilizing rigs are restricted at INAH sites and are commonly refused at the entrance without prior authorization. The reasoning is crowd flow and monument protection rather than image quality. In practice this means a permitted commercial shoot can bring support gear listed on the permit, and everyone else is shooting handheld. Plan for handheld work and fast lenses if you are visiting as a regular guest.
Can we fly a drone over Chichen Itza or the Tulum ruins?
Not without written federal authorization, which recreational and wedding shoots are not granted. Archaeological zones are restricted airspace under INAH and civil aviation rules, security staff at the major sites actively watch for drones, and the realistic outcomes are confiscation of the aircraft and a fine. We do not fly over any archaeological zone, and we would encourage you to be wary of any vendor who offers to.
Where can we get the ruins look without the permit problem?
Cenotes with exposed limestone, the jungle roads between Tulum and Cobá, resort properties built around Mayan-inspired stone, private haciendas near Valladolid, and the raw dune and rock of the Tulum beach road all deliver the texture couples are really asking for. Stone, shadow, green canopy and Caribbean light are the ingredients. The specific monument is rarely the thing people remember in the album.
