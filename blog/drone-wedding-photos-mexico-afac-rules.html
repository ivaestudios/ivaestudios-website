In short

Commercial drone flight at a Mexican wedding requires AFAC authorisation, which goes to Mexican operators; a US Part 107 certificate has no legal standing in Mexico. Under NOM-107 flights are daylight only, within visual line of sight, below roughly 122 metres, away from airports and never over people outside the operation, and INAH archaeological zones are off-limits without a permit.

Drone footage is on almost every couple's mood board and it is the single most misunderstood item on a Mexican wedding day. The confusion is understandable: the rules are Mexican, the photographers are often not, and the person who ends up explaining it is usually a resort coordinator two weeks before the wedding. Here is the honest set of facts, from a Mexican owned studio that flies here, including the licence question, the twelve person rule, the places you legally cannot fly, and a frank assessment of what a drone actually adds to a beach ceremony gallery.

Who Regulates Drones in Mexico and What AFAC Requires

Civil aviation in Mexico is regulated by AFAC, the Agencia Federal de Aviación Civil, which succeeded the old DGAC. Drones, or in the regulatory language remotely piloted aircraft systems, are governed principally by the standard known as NOM-107, which sets out how they may be operated in Mexican airspace.

The framework classifies aircraft by weight. Very light aircraft under two kilograms face the lightest requirements, aircraft between two and twenty five kilograms carry more, and anything heavier is a different world entirely. Most wedding drones sit in the light categories. Registration is required above a low weight threshold, and the standard operating conditions apply across the board: daylight only, within visual line of sight, below roughly 122 metres of altitude, away from aerodromes, and not over people who are not part of the operation.

The part that matters most for weddings is the distinction between recreational and commercial flight. A commercial operation, which is what a paid photographer flying at your wedding is, requires authorisation from AFAC, and that authorisation goes to Mexican operators. This is not a technicality invented to be annoying. It is how the country regulates commercial aviation activity.

The short version

A drone at your Mexican wedding is legal, common and often beautiful. It is legal when flown by an operator authorised in Mexico, offset from the guests rather than over them, away from restricted airspace and archaeological zones, and with the property's written permission. Everything below is the detail behind that sentence.

Why Your Fly-In Photographer's Part 107 Does Not Apply

This comes up constantly and it deserves a direct answer. A United States FAA Part 107 remote pilot certificate has no legal standing in Mexico. It is a national certificate issued under United States law for operations in United States airspace, and Mexico does not recognise it as authorisation to conduct commercial drone operations here. The same is true of a European or Canadian equivalent.

What that means practically is that a photographer flying in from abroad for your wedding, however skilled and however insured at home, is generally not in a position to legally conduct a commercial drone flight in Mexico. Many do it anyway. The risk is not usually a dramatic enforcement scene; it is that a resort's security stops the flight, or that a local operator flags it, and the aerial footage you paid for does not exist. Occasionally it is worse, and equipment is seized.

If aerials matter to you, the reliable path is to have them flown by a Mexican operator with AFAC authorisation, either the studio or a local pilot the studio brings in and coordinates with. That is how we handle it, and it is one of the practical advantages of hiring locally that nobody puts on a website. Our about page covers how the studio is set up.

Gathering-Size Limits and What That Means at a Ceremony

This is the rule that surprises everyone. Mexican drone regulation restricts flight over concentrations of people, and it defines a concentration as twelve or more people. Twelve. Which means that in practice, essentially any wedding ceremony with guests counts, and flying directly over the aisle while your family is seated in it is not a legal operation.

That does not kill the shot list. It reshapes it. What we fly is offset: parallel to the ceremony rather than above it, from the water side looking back at the shore, high and wide so the gathering is a small element in a large landscape, or before and after the ceremony when guests are not assembled. The frames people actually want, the couple alone on an enormous beach, the reef colour, the resort in context, the aisle seen from an angle, are all achievable without flying over anybody's head.

It also means the drone should not be the reason the ceremony is designed a particular way. We fly it around the wedding. The wedding does not perform for it.

Insurance, Registration and the Operator Certificate

Three documents come up when a serious property asks questions, and any operator who cannot produce them should not be flying at your wedding.

The first is registration of the aircraft itself, required above the low weight threshold. The second is the pilot's authorisation to operate, which for commercial work means the AFAC permission rather than a hobbyist's assumption. The third is third party liability insurance, which is the one resorts care about most, because their concern is a drone falling into a wedding, not paperwork philosophy.

Resorts increasingly ask for these in advance, and larger chains in the Hotel Zone and along the Riviera Maya often have a standing policy requiring written approval before any flight on their property. Ask your coordinator for the property's drone policy at the same time you ask about outside vendor fees. If the answer is that drones are not permitted, that is the property's right and it ends the conversation regardless of what anyone's licence says.

No-Fly Reality: Airport Airspace and INAH Zones

Two categories of restriction bite hardest on this coast.

Airport airspace. The rules keep drones well clear of aerodromes, with a buffer measured in kilometres rather than metres. Cancún International is one of the busiest airports in Latin America and sits just south of the Hotel Zone, so the southern end of the strip and the corridor toward Puerto Morelos require checking rather than assuming. Since December 2023 there is a second consideration in the south, because the Felipe Carrillo Puerto airport southwest of Tulum created controlled airspace where there was none before. Any operator flying here should be checking the current charts for your specific venue, not relying on what was true two years ago.

Archaeological zones. INAH, the Instituto Nacional de Antropología e Historia, prohibits drone flights over archaeological sites without a specific permit. That covers Tulum, Cobá, Chichén Itzá, El Rey and the rest. Confiscations and fines at Tulum are documented, and the site staff are attentive. This matters for weddings because couples frequently want a portrait session at or near the ruins, and the answer on the aerial is no unless a permit has been obtained through the proper process, which is not a same week undertaking.

There are also situational restrictions: temporary flight restrictions during official visits, and the reasonable prohibition on flying over private property or other hotels without permission. A drone that drifts over the neighbouring resort's pool deck is a complaint waiting to happen.

Where Aerials Are Actually Legal in the Riviera Maya

Plenty of places, which is the good news. Open coastline away from airport buffers and archaeological zones is generally workable with property permission: much of the Riviera Maya resort corridor, Playa Mujeres and the coast north of Cancún, Isla Mujeres, and large parts of the Mayakoba and Puerto Aventuras stretch. Beaches with a wide open aspect and no protected site nearby are the easiest cases.

The harder cases are the Tulum beach road, which now sits between an archaeological zone and a new airport, and the southern Hotel Zone near Cancún airport. Neither is an automatic no, but both need checking for your exact coordinates and date rather than a general assurance. "A drone is a landscape instrument. It tells the viewer where you were. It almost never tells them how the day felt."

What a Drone Adds to a Beach Ceremony Gallery, Honestly

We fly, we like flying, and we will still tell you this: the drone is a supporting instrument, not the main one. In a finished wedding gallery, aerials usually earn three or four frames. They establish the place, they show the reef and the colour gradient of the water that no ground level camera captures, and they give one wide overhead of the ceremony geometry that people genuinely enjoy.

What they do not do is portraits. A face at forty metres is a dot. Emotion lives at eighty five millimetres from three metres away, and no aerial has ever replaced the frame of someone's mother crying. There is also a noise cost. A drone hovering near a beach ceremony is audible and it changes the atmosphere of vows, which is why we fly before the processional and after the recessional rather than during the exchange itself.

Our honest recommendation for most couples: include aerials, keep the expectation to a handful of establishing frames, and do not let the aerial shot list dictate the ceremony design or the timeline. If a wide coastal frame is what you are really after, a long lens from the right position at the right hour often delivers more feeling than an overhead ever will.
