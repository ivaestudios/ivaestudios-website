Rain and Wind: The Couples Session Backup Plan

In short

We almost never cancel for rain: a Caribbean storm is a twenty to forty minute event and the washed light after it is often the best of the week. For a golden hour session the call comes by 2 PM, read from the radar loop rather than the forecast percentage. The real enemy is wind, not rain.

Every couple who books a session between June and November asks the same question, usually in the same slightly apologetic tone: what happens if it rains? The short answer is that we almost never cancel, because a Caribbean afternoon storm is a twenty to forty minute event and not a day. The longer and more useful answer is that rain is not the problem people think it is. Wind is. A grey sky is a giant softbox and it flatters everybody. A twenty knot northerly puts hair across your face in ninety percent of the frames and there is no plugin for that. So this is the operational plan: how the decision gets made, what a wet session actually looks like when you see the gallery, the six covered locations that exist at almost every resort on this coast, and how the studio handles moving a date.

How We Decide at 2 PM Whether to Move

The first thing to understand about weather here is the shape of it. A typical Quintana Roo storm in the wet season builds inland over the peninsula through the afternoon, drifts east toward the coast, drops hard for twenty to forty minutes, and then it is over. The light after it is frequently the best light of the week, because the air is washed clean and the cloud edges catch the low sun. So the decision is almost never cancel. It is move, or wait.

The second thing is that the percentage on your phone is close to useless for this. A sixty percent chance of rain means that sixty percent of the forecast area is expected to get rain at some point in the period. It does not mean your evening is gone, and couples cancel plans over that number every week for no reason.

What we actually look at is the radar loop, because it shows a cell's size, its direction and its speed. That tells you whether the thing on the horizon will be over your beach in twenty minutes and gone by six, or whether a broad band is sitting on the coast for the evening. We use the Servicio Meteorológico Nacional radar, a proper looping radar app, and the resort concierge, who watches this sky for a living and is usually right.

For a 6:00 PM session the call gets made by 2:00 PM, and it comes in three flavours. If a small cell is moving through, we hold the time and expect to start slightly late. If a broad band is stalled over the coast, we start on time and go straight to the covered plan. If a tropical wave is parked over the peninsula for the evening, we reschedule. You get told either way by two o'clock, because you need time to decide what to wear and to change your dinner reservation.

The number that matters

The forecast percentage tells you about the area, not about your hour. Look at a radar loop instead. On this coast a cell that is fifteen kilometres wide and moving east at twenty kilometres an hour is a forty minute inconvenience, not a cancelled session.

What a Rain-Day Session Actually Looks Like in Photos

Overcast is the most flattering light there is and it is the reason studio photographers spend money on large diffusers. A solid cloud layer is one enormous soft source directly overhead. Nobody squints. There are no hard shadows under eyes or noses. Skin renders evenly, highlights do not blow out, and the dynamic range collapses into something a camera handles beautifully.

Actual falling rain only reads in a photograph if it is backlit or set against something dark, so we look for a light behind the drops: a lamp, a lit doorway, the last of the sun under a cloud edge. Without that the rain is invisible and the picture just looks soft.

The honest part is this: on a wet session you will either be wet or you will be under a roof, and those are the only two options. We ask which one you want before we start rather than finding out halfway through, and about a third of couples pick wet.

Wind: Hair, Dresses, Sand and What It Does to a Frame

Cancún runs a daily sea breeze that builds through the morning, peaks in the early afternoon and usually drops near sunset. Then there is the norte season, roughly November through February, when north winds can hold at fifteen to twenty five knots for days at a time under a perfectly blue sky. That is the situation nobody plans for, because the forecast says sunny and the beach is unusable.

What wind actually does to a session: hair goes across the face in most frames and stays there, a light dress wraps around legs instead of flowing, sand travels at ankle height and gets into eyes and onto front elements, and voices do not carry, which matters more than you would expect because direction is most of the job.

What we do about it is mostly geometry. We work with the wind at an angle rather than head on so that hair blows off the face and away from the camera rather than across it. We use it deliberately for movement frames, because a veil or a long skirt downwind is a gift. And when it is too much, we move: behind a dune, behind a wall, into a palm line, along the lee side of a building, or into a colonnade where it stops entirely.

What you can do is small and effective. Bring hair pins and a thin elastic. Choose one outfit with some weight to the hem. If you are visiting between November and February, do not plan the entire session around a strapless chiffon dress without a structured alternative in the bag. And remember that the last hour before sunset is usually the calmest hour on the water, which is a second reason golden hour is the studio rule.

Six Covered Locations at Almost Any Resort

This is the list we walk when we scout a property, and some version of all six exists at nearly every resort between Cancún and Tulum.

One, the arrival colonnade. The covered drop off at the front of the hotel is a large roof with open sides, which means huge soft light entering from both ends and a guaranteed dry floor. It is architecturally the grandest space on most properties and almost nobody photographs in it.

Two, the lobby. Tall glass, a polished floor that reflects, and the part of the building the resort actually spent money on. Best in the hour before dinner service when it is quiet.

Three, a covered restaurant terrace or palapa. Warm wood or thatch overhead, open toward the sea, and a view without the rain. Use it before service starts and ask the manager first.

Four, the spa or a water feature courtyard. Quiet, usually symmetrical, calm water, deep shade, and staff who are already used to people being silent in it.

Five, a covered arcade or walkway between buildings. Repeating arches or columns are the single most reliable graphic element on any resort property, and a wet floor under them reflects.

Six, a corridor or stairwell with a window at one end. One directional light source with a dark surround gives the most editorial frame on the whole property, and it costs nothing and needs nobody's permission.

On permissions: the colonnade and the lobby are usually fine for guests. A restaurant before service normally needs a word with the manager, and the spa needs a word with the spa. We ask in Spanish, on the day, and most properties say yes. A small number have a photography policy that restricts outside photographers to certain areas or requires their in house team, which is worth checking with your resort before you book anything, not after.

Colonnades, Lobbies, Terraces and the In-Suite Fallback

When everything outdoors is genuinely gone, the suite is not a defeat. It is a different and often better session, and it is the one couples are most surprised by.

The recipe is consistent. Turn every lamp in the room off, because mixing warm tungsten with cool daylight is the thing that makes hotel room photographs look like hotel room photographs. One window becomes the only light. Move furniture out of the frame rather than composing around it, since a chair takes four seconds to shift. Shoot toward the window for silhouette and rim light, and across it for soft modelled portraits.

Then use the architecture a room actually has: the doorway between two rooms as a frame, the mirror, the sheer curtain as a diffuser you can pull across, the shower glass, and the balcony overhang, which is a covered, private, elevated space with the view you paid for.

Rescheduling Policy and How Many Days You Need Spare

Our policy in plain words. If we make the call to move for weather, we move to a covered plan at no extra cost. If you would rather reschedule, we move to any other day in your trip that is open on our calendar, also at no extra cost. If nothing on either side of your date is open, we return the deposit. We do not charge weather fees and we do not shoot a session neither of us believes in just to close the booking.

What that means for how you plan. Book the session for the middle of your trip rather than the last afternoon. Two spare days is the comfortable number and one works most of the time. Zero spare days means we shoot in whatever the sky gives us on that specific evening, and most evenings are fine, but that is a coin flip you chose to take.

A wedding day is a different problem, because it cannot move. That is exactly why we plan every wedding with an indoor ceremony option and a named covered portrait location on paper, weeks before the week arrives. If your resort has no covered ceremony space, that is something to know while you can still choose a different resort.

Why the Weather Sessions Are Often the Favourites

Something happens to people when the plan breaks. The performance stops. Nobody is trying to look like the picture they saw on Pinterest, because that picture had sunshine in it and this is not that. What we get instead is two people laughing at the situation, running for a doorway, sharing an umbrella badly, and those frames are consistently the closest and the funniest of the set.

We say this having delivered more than 500 sessions on a coast with a six month wet season, and it is not a consolation line: no couple has ever come back and asked to reshoot a weather session. Several have asked us to print the rain frame.

Common Questions About Weather and Rescheduling

What happens if it rains on the day of our session?

We move rather than cancel. By around 2:00 PM on the day we look at the radar loop and tell you the plan: hold the time and expect a late start if a cell is passing through, start on time under cover if a band has settled in, or reschedule if the whole evening is gone. A Caribbean afternoon storm usually lasts twenty to forty minutes, and the light after one is often the best of the week.

Do you cancel for wind?

Very rarely, but wind changes where we shoot. Sustained wind over about fifteen knots means hair across faces, a dress that wraps instead of flows, and sand at ankle height. We work with the wind at an angle so hair blows off the face, use it deliberately for movement frames, and move behind a dune, a wall, a palm line or into a colonnade. Bring pins and a hair elastic, and a second structured outfit if you are here in the norte season between November and February.

Can we reschedule, and how much notice do you need?

If we make the call to move for weather, we move to a covered plan at no extra cost, or we reschedule to any other day in your trip that is open on our calendar, also at no extra cost. If nothing on either side of your date is open, we refund the deposit. Tell us as early as you can if your own plans change, because our calendar is capped and dates fill.

Is hurricane season a bad time to book a session?

Not bad, just less forgiving. Atlantic hurricane season runs from 1 June to 30 November and September and October are the wettest months here. Most days still have a usable evening, and rates and crowds are both lower. The single thing that matters is slack in your itinerary: book the session for the middle of the trip rather than the last afternoon, and leave one or two spare days.

Where do you shoot if the whole day is wet?

Six places exist at almost every resort on this coast: the arrival colonnade, the lobby, a covered restaurant terrace or palapa, the spa or a water feature courtyard, a covered arcade between buildings, and a corridor or stairwell with a window at one end. If all of those are unavailable, your own suite with every lamp switched off and one window as the light source produces the most intimate set most couples get.

Do rain photos look bad?

The opposite, usually. Cloud is one enormous soft light source, so nobody squints and skin renders evenly. Wet stone, wood and sand saturate and reflect. The beach empties. Rain itself only shows in a frame when it is backlit, so we look for a light behind the drops. We have never had a couple ask to reshoot a weather session.
