Family Photos With Less Mobile Grandparents

In short

Entirely possible with planning: we photograph the full group and the grandparent portraits first, favour boardwalks, pool decks and hard-packed shoreline over soft sand, and treat seated portraits as the design rather than a compromise. Plan for a 60 to 75 minute session with the grandparent involved for the first 20 to 30 minutes.

Multigenerational travel is the top luxury travel trend of 2026, with the large majority of travel advisors reporting more grandparent, parent and child trips than in any previous year. What almost nobody writes about is the constraint that quietly decides whether the family photograph happens at all: soft sand, heat and a grandparent who uses a walker. Families ask us about this in a lowered voice, as though it were an imposition. It is not. It is a planning problem with good answers, and this is how we solve it.

Sand and wheels: what is actually possible

Start with the honest part. A standard wheelchair does not work on soft dry sand. The narrow wheels sink, the chair stops, and pushing harder just tips it. This is true of every beach on this coast and no amount of goodwill changes the physics. The same applies to most walkers, whose small feet sink and catch, and to anyone unsteady on their feet, because dry sand shifts underfoot in a way that is genuinely unsafe for a fragile hip.

Now the useful part. Three surfaces do work, and between them they cover almost every photograph a family actually wants.

The first is hard-packed wet sand near the waterline, especially at low tide. It is firm enough for a standard chair to be pushed and stable enough to walk on with a cane. The catch is getting to it across the soft dry band above, which is where a second pair of hands and a planned route matter. The second is any built surface: boardwalks, beach walkways, terraces, pool decks. The third is a beach wheelchair, the kind with wide balloon tyres, which some resorts keep for guests and which changes what is possible completely if the property has one.

Ask before you arrive

Email your resort and ask whether they have a beach wheelchair available to guests, and whether the boardwalk reaches the sand or stops short of it. Those two answers determine our entire session plan. Properties that have one will usually reserve it for a specific time if you ask a few days ahead.

Boardwalks, pool decks and hard-packed shoreline

Here is the thing families do not believe until they see the pictures. You do not need to be far out on the sand for a photograph to look like a beach photograph.

What puts the ocean behind a family is the camera angle, not the family's position. From the end of a boardwalk, from a terrace, from the firm top edge of the beach, a low camera position places the horizon behind everybody and the sand out of frame. The photograph reads as sea and sky. Nobody looking at it later has any idea that the group was standing on a wooden deck.

Pool decks are the underrated option. Most resorts here put their infinity pools facing the water, so a deck edge gives you sea, horizon and a clean architectural line, on flat ground with seating and shade nearby.

"The horizon is decided by where the camera is, not by where the family is standing. Once you understand that, most accessibility constraints stop being photographic constraints."

Seated portraits that are not a compromise

There is a version of the seated family portrait that looks like an accommodation, and there is a version that looks like the best photograph of the evening. The difference is whether it was planned.

The version that works starts from the opposite direction. The seated person is placed first, at the centre, and the family is built around them. Some people crouch, some kneel, children sit on the ground in front, one or two stand behind with a hand on a shoulder. The heights become a deliberate composition rather than a problem. We shoot from lower so the seated person is not looked down on, which matters enormously for how the photograph feels, and we get in close enough that the group reads as one connected shape rather than a row.

Two more things that help. First, hands. Contact between generations, a grandchild's hand on an arm, a son's hand on a shoulder, does more emotional work in these photographs than any pose. Second, chairs. If the seated person uses a wheelchair, we ask whether they would prefer to be photographed in it or transferred to a bench or a chair from the restaurant, and we do whatever they say. Some people want the chair in the picture because it is part of their life. Others do not. It is their decision and nobody else's, and we ask them directly rather than asking their family.

Heat, shade and how often we sit everyone down

Heat and mobility compound each other. An eighty year old standing on warm sand in 31 degree heat is doing far more work than the rest of the family realises, and some common medications, including several blood pressure treatments and diuretics, change how the body copes with heat and fluid. We are not medical professionals and we do not need details. We just need to know that someone should not be out for long.

So the plan always includes a seat. Not a theoretical seat somewhere in the resort, an actual chair placed at the location before the session starts, in shade, with cold water on it. If the spot has no natural shade, we ask the resort for an umbrella. Twenty steps is our rule: shade and water within twenty steps of wherever we are working.

Session length follows from that. Sixty to seventy five minutes total is right, with the grandparent involved for the first twenty to thirty. Our full heat plan for a Cancún family session goes through timing, hydration and clothing in more detail, and it applies doubly when mobility is a factor.

Grouping order so the oldest person shoots first

Our order is: the whole group first, immediately, in the first ten minutes while the light is still workable and everyone is fresh and nobody has wandered off. Then the grandparents alone, then the grandparents with the grandchildren, then the grandparents with each of their adult children. By minute twenty five, every photograph involving the oldest generation exists. They can then sit in the shade and watch, or go back to the room, and nothing is lost.

The rest of the session, the nuclear families, the cousins, the couples, happens afterwards in the warmest light. Younger people can absorb another forty minutes of heat easily. This ordering is why our sessions with less mobile grandparents almost never run into trouble: the important frames are already made before anyone is tired. We use the same structure for large groups generally, described on our luxury family photography page.

Resorts with the best accessible beach access

We deliberately will not publish a ranked list of accessible resorts, because facilities change, a beach wheelchair that existed last season may be broken this one.

What we will give you is the test. Ask the resort three questions, by email, and keep the reply. Does a boardwalk or ramp reach the sand, or does it stop at the top of the beach. Do you have a beach wheelchair available to guests, and can it be reserved. Is there a terrace, deck or restaurant with an unobstructed sea view and step-free access. Three answers, and we can plan a session precisely.

If your resort turns out to have nothing suitable, there are usually alternatives within a short drive, including public promenades and marina areas with flat paved access and a genuine sea view. We will tell you honestly whether the alternative is as good or a compromise. The properties we already know well are listed on our venues page, and if yours is not there we will scout it before your date.

Talking to the family beforehand without making it awkward

The last piece is the human one, and it is the reason most families do not raise this at all.

Tell us privately. A message with three facts is enough: who it is, what they use, and roughly how long they are comfortable being out. You do not need to explain a diagnosis and we will not ask. What that message buys is that on the day nothing is discussed in front of anyone. The chair is already there. The route is already flat. The running order already puts them first. Nobody announces an accommodation, because it was made two weeks earlier.

One thing worth saying plainly. Families sometimes hesitate over these sessions because they are afraid the photographs will document decline. In our experience the opposite happens. Ten years later nobody looks at that gallery and sees a walker. They see the last time everyone was in one place, and they are grateful somebody organised it. That is a reason to book the session, not a reason to postpone it.

Questions families ask

Can a wheelchair go on the beach in Cancún?

A standard wheelchair does not work on soft dry sand. Narrow wheels sink within a metre or two and pushing becomes unsafe. What does work is hard-packed wet sand near the waterline at low tide, boardwalks and beach walkways, and pool decks and terraces overlooking the sea. Some resorts have beach wheelchairs with wide balloon tyres available on request, which change what is possible completely.

Can we still get photos with the ocean behind us?

Yes, and this is the part families worry about most unnecessarily. From the end of a boardwalk, a terrace or the firm sand at the top of the beach, the frame still reads as ocean and sky. What decides whether the sea is behind you is the angle we shoot from and how low we place the camera, not how far onto the sand anyone walked.

Do seated portraits look like a compromise?

They do not, when they are planned rather than improvised. A seated grandparent with the family gathered close and leaning in reads as warm and deliberate. The version that looks like a compromise is a standing line-up with one person parked slightly apart at the end, which is what happens when nobody planned for it. We build the group around the seated person from the start.

How long can a session be if a grandparent tires easily?

Plan for the whole session to run 60 to 75 minutes, with the grandparent involved for the first 20 to 30 of them. We photograph the oldest generation first, while they are freshest, then they can sit in the shade or return to the room while the rest of the family continues. Nothing important is missed by leaving early because the important frames happen first.

Which resorts have the best accessible beach access?

Rather than name properties whose facilities change, the reliable test is to ask the resort three questions: is there a boardwalk or ramp that reaches the sand, do you have a beach wheelchair available to guests, and is there a terrace or deck with an unobstructed sea view. Newer large resorts in Costa Mujeres and the Riviera Maya generally answer better than older Hotel Zone properties, and the calm shallow shoreline north of Cancún is easier terrain.

How do we raise this with the family without it being awkward?

Tell us privately rather than negotiating it in front of everyone. A short message with the person's name, what they use and roughly how long they can be comfortable is enough. On the day nothing is announced, the chair is simply already there and the running order already puts them first. Handled quietly in advance, it never becomes a moment.
