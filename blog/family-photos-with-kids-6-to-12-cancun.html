In short

Kids 6 to 12 smile for real when they are busy, not when they are asked. Give each child a job, a challenge or a spot to choose, skip “say cheese,” and give a self-conscious tween a real say. By the time kids start school, most of them have learned what a camera wants. The phone comes out, the shoulders go up, and the smile arrives on cue: all teeth, no eyes. Family photos with older kids are a joy: kids between 6 and 12 can race, dig, tease a sibling and land a joke, and they are old enough to have opinions, which is why they need a different plan from a toddler. Here is how we get real faces from school-age kids on a Cancun beach, and what you can say, and skip, to help.

Where the Cheese Smile Comes From

The fake smile is not bad behavior. It is a skill. After years of “say cheese,” school-age kids learn to produce a quick, polite performance that switches off the moment the camera drops. Ask for it again and you get it again, a little stiffer every time.

A real smile reaches the eyes, and it rarely comes on command. It shows up when a kid is busy, proud, surprised or trying very hard not to laugh. So we stop asking for it. Instead of pointing the lens at a child and requesting a smile, we give them something to do or someone to react to, and we photograph what happens.

Ask a nine-year-old for a smile and you get the cheese. Give them a job and you get the kid.

Their attention has a shape, too. A seven-year-old can play on the beach for an hour, but patience for anything that feels like posing often runs out after about twenty minutes. That is why we take the family portrait early, while everyone is fresh, and change the activity before boredom sets in. If a toddler is coming along too, our tips for family photos with toddlers cover the little one's side of the hour.

Turn Them Into Helpers

A kid who is helping is not posing. Between 6 and 12, the quickest way to a real face is a role in the session: something to do, something to win or something to choose. Here are three family photo ideas with kids this age.

A job

Give each child a real responsibility. Hold the little one's hand on the walk down the beach. Find the best shell for a close-up of everyone's hands. Watch the sea and call out when a big wave is coming, so the whole family jumps at once. Older siblings make great assistant directors: a ten-year-old put in charge of making a four-year-old laugh can hand us two real smiles in one frame.

A challenge

School-age kids will do almost anything for a contest. Race to the waterline and back. Freeze like a statue until the wave touches your toes. See who can jump highest when the foam rolls in. And for the fake smile itself, try a no-smiling contest: the first one to smile loses. Nobody lasts long, and the laugh that breaks through is the real one.

A spot that is theirs

Before the session, let each child choose one spot, or one shot, that belongs to them: a palm tree, the place where the waves break, a frame of just the siblings. When we get there, they lead the way. A kid leading us to a spot they picked is not being dragged along; they are showing us something. Our guide on how to let the kids pick the family session location shows how to hand over the choice without giving up good light.

Tell us at booking

Each child's age, what they love (water, sand, racing, animals), who would rather skip the photos and the one spot or shot each of them wants. We plan the hour around those answers.

What to Say, and What to Skip

Much of what happens in front of the camera is decided in the hotel room, and a few words can do more than any pose.

Before the session

  • Say how long it lasts and what comes next. “One hour of photos on the beach, then ice cream” gives them a finish line they can picture, which a vague “it'll be quick” never does. Keep the promise small, specific and real.
  • Say what their job is, and let them pick their spot.
  • Skip the pressure. “You'd better smile” or “these photos cost a lot” is the fastest route to the cheese smile. Don't practice smiles in the mirror, either.
  • Skip the brand-new outfit. Let them pick one piece they like within the family palette.

During the session

  • Say less. Let us do the directing, so you can be in the photos instead of producing them.
  • Talk to your kid, not about them. Ask about the best thing they did this week, or whisper a terrible joke. Real questions get real faces.
  • Skip “say cheese,” “smile with your teeth” and “look at the camera.” Calling out from behind us pulls their eyes off the lens and brings the stiff smile right back.
  • Skip “stop being silly.” At this age, silly is often the best frame of the hour.
  • Skip comments on hair, braces or clothes in front of everyone. Kids this age hear every word.
Try this the night before

“Tomorrow we're doing photos on the beach for one hour. You've got a job, and you get to pick one spot. Afterwards, we're getting ice cream.” Then keep the promise.

Ten to Twelve: Old Enough to Have Opinions

Younger school-age kids are usually happy to play for the camera. Somewhere around 10 to 12, some start to care a lot about how they look and who is watching. That is normal, not attitude: the American Academy of Pediatrics describes early adolescence as ages 10 to 13 and notes on HealthyChildren.org that preteens often feel self-conscious about how they look (checked November 14, 2026). Tween family photos on the beach go best when you plan for it:

  • Ask in private, before the trip. Which spot would they choose, and which photo would they actually want to keep? Asked at home, the question gets a real answer. Asked on the sand in front of their siblings, it gets a shrug.
  • Give them status, not little-kid games. A ten-year-old who feels too old for a splash race will often happily run one: counting down the wave, judging the jump, naming the winner.
  • Mention sore spots quietly. New glasses, a growth spurt or a haircut they regret: if it matters to them, tell us when you book, not in front of them.
  • Promise them a say in what gets posted. It costs nothing and buys a lot of trust.

Pairing them with a younger sibling helps too: a big kid looking after a little one tends to forget to be cool. From 13, our guide to family photos with teenagers takes over, and for a child of any age who simply is not in the mood, see our guide for shy or uncooperative kids.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I get my kids to stop fake smiling in photos?

Stop asking for a smile. The fake smile is a performance kids learn from years of “say cheese,” and asking again only makes it stiffer. Give them a job, a challenge such as a no-smiling contest, or a spot they chose, and we photograph what happens while they are busy.

What should I tell my kids before a family photo session?

Tell them how long it lasts, what their job is and what happens afterwards, for example one hour on the beach and then ice cream. Skip pressure such as “you'd better smile,” and don't practice smiles in the mirror.

My 11-year-old hates having their picture taken. What helps?

A real say, asked in private before the trip: which spot they would choose and which photo they want to keep. Give them a role with some status, such as judging a jump contest, mention anything they feel self-conscious about when you book rather than in front of them, and promise them a say in which photos get posted.

How long should a family session with kids 6 to 12 be?

For most families we recommend The Chapter: 60 minutes, 35 edited photos and one look for 475 USD. Patience for posing often runs out after about twenty minutes at this age, so we take the family portrait early and keep changing activities.

What time of day is best for family photos with school-age kids in Cancun?

Online booking offers start times of 6:30, 7:00 and 7:30 am and 5:00 and 5:30 pm; other times can be arranged on WhatsApp. Early slots suit early risers and kids who don't want an audience; late afternoon suits slow starters.
