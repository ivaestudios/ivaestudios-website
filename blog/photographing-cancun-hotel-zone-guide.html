Photographing the Cancun Hotel Zone, Kilometre by Kilometre

In short

The Hotel Zone is a narrow barrier island with the Caribbean on one side and the Nichupté lagoon on the other, and everything is addressed by kilometre along one road. It faces east, so the sun rises out of the sea in front of the beach and sets behind it over the lagoon. That single fact decides your hour: sunrise on the sand, or the last ninety minutes of afternoon in open shade with bright water still in front of you. Never noon.

How the strip is laid out

Cancun is really two cities. There is downtown, where people live, and there is the Hotel Zone, a barrier island roughly twenty two kilometres long, shaped like the number seven, joined to the mainland by bridges at each end. One road runs its whole length: Boulevard Kukulcan.

Nothing here has a street address that anyone uses. Everything is given as a kilometre. Your resort will tell you it is at Km 14.5, your driver will know exactly where that is, and so will we. When you send us your hotel, the first thing we work out is the kilometre, because it decides which beach you walk to and how far anyone has to go in the heat.

The Caribbean is on the east side of the island. The Nichupté lagoon, a shallow mangrove system, is on the west. In the space between them sits everything: the resorts, the restaurants, the marinas and, at the elbow of the seven, the strip of nightlife most visitors picture when they hear the name.

Why the direction matters more than the spot

Here is the thing almost nobody is told before they book. The beach faces east. The sun comes up out of the water in front of you and goes down behind you, over the lagoon, where no session is ever set up.

Couples arrive picturing a sunset on the sand, because that is what they saw online. Those photographs were almost always made on a west facing coast, the Pacific side of Mexico or the Caribbean islands that happen to sit the other way round. Here, a four o'clock session on the beach puts the sun high and behind the group, and the light on your faces is the light bouncing back off white sand, which is the harshest this coast gives.

So the hour is not a preference, it is the whole decision. Two windows work. Sunrise, from about half past six, when the sun lifts straight out of the Caribbean in front of you, the sand has just been raked and the beach is empty. And the last ninety minutes of the afternoon, when the sun has dropped behind the buildings and the beach sits in open shade with the bright water still in front of it. That second window is the one most families can actually get out of the room for.

Twenty minutes after sunset there is a third, shorter window that people forget: the sky over the lagoon at your back holds colour long after the beach has gone dark. It is worth stepping out of dinner for.

The stretches, north to south

The island is not one beach. It changes character as you move along it.

The northern arm, roughly Km 1 to Km 8. This is the short leg of the seven, and it is the only part that faces north, toward Isla Mujeres rather than the open sea. The water is calmer and shallower, the waves are gentler, and it is where families with small children tend to stay. Because it points a different way, the light behaves differently here than on the rest of the strip.

Punta Cancun, around Km 9. The bend. Marinas, the convention centre, the busiest restaurants and the loudest nights. Good for a session only very early, before the strip wakes up.

The long eastern run, Km 10 to Km 20. This is the classic Cancun beach: open Caribbean, real surf, and the resorts most visitors picture. The Ritz-Carlton, Le Blanc, Kempinski, the JW Marriott and their neighbours sit along here. Wide sand, long horizons, nothing in the frame but water.

Playa Delfines, near Km 18. Public, high on a bluff, and home to the giant coloured CANCUN letters. It gives you the postcard view down the whole coastline. It is also the busiest photo spot on the island, so it works at sunrise and rarely at any other hour.

Punta Nizuc, past Km 21. The southern tip, quieter, reef close to shore, and the point where the Hotel Zone gives way to the road toward the airport.

Public beach, private resort

Every beach in Mexico is federal land up to the tide line. No hotel owns the sand. What a resort controls is its access, its furniture and its rules about who may work on its property.

In practice this means two separate questions. The first is whether we can photograph on the beach in front of your hotel, and the answer is almost always yes. The second is whether the resort charges an outside vendor fee or asks the photographer to register, and that varies a great deal from property to property.

Some of the larger all-inclusive brands have an in-house studio and apply a fee to anyone else. Others wave you through with a name at the desk. A few ask only that we do not use the pool deck or the lobby. We handle that conversation with your coordinator in Spanish before you land, so it is settled rather than discovered on the morning.

Sargassum, wind and the things you cannot book

Two things on this coast are outside anyone's control, and it is better to know them in advance than to be surprised.

Sargassum. The seaweed arrives on the eastern side of the peninsula roughly from spring into early autumn, and it varies week to week and even beach to beach. Nobody can promise you a clear shoreline months ahead, and anyone who does is guessing. What is reliable is that resorts rake their stretch every morning, so an early session sees the sand at its cleanest. The northern arm, facing Isla Mujeres rather than the open sea, is often less affected than the long eastern run.

Wind. The trade wind comes off the water most afternoons. It moves veils, hair and anything unpinned. We plan around it rather than fight it, and honestly, hair in the wind on an open beach is usually the better photograph.

Neither of these is a reason to change your dates. They are reasons to choose your hour well, which brings the whole thing back to the same decision.

Frequently Asked Questions

What time of day is best for photos in the Cancun Hotel Zone?

Sunrise, from around half past six, or the last ninety minutes before sunset. The beach faces east, so at sunrise the sun rises out of the water in front of you, and in late afternoon the sun has dropped behind the buildings and the beach sits in open shade with bright water still in front. Midday is the one hour to avoid: the sun is overhead and the white sand throws hard light straight up under the eyes.

Can I have a sunset beach ceremony in the Hotel Zone?

Not with the sun setting over the sea, because Cancun's beach faces east. The sun sets behind the strip, over the Nichupte lagoon. For a sun going down into the water you want Playa Norte on Isla Mujeres, about twenty minutes away by ferry, which is the one nearby beach that faces west.

Is the beach in front of my resort private?

No beach in Mexico is private. The sand up to the tide line is federal land. Your resort controls its access, its chairs and its own rules for outside vendors, but not the shoreline itself. In practice a resort stretch is quiet, and we frame to keep other guests out of your photographs.

Do Cancun resorts allow outside photographers?

Most do, but the terms vary widely. Some charge an outside vendor fee, some ask the photographer to register at the desk, and a few restrict certain areas such as the pool deck or lobby. We confirm the policy with your coordinator in Spanish before you arrive so there are no surprises on the day.

Where is the CANCUN sign and is it good for photos?

The large coloured letters are at Playa Delfines, near kilometre 18, on a bluff with a long view down the coast. It is the single busiest photo spot on the island, so it works well at sunrise and is usually crowded at every other hour.

Which part of the Hotel Zone has the calmest water?

The northern arm, roughly kilometres 1 to 8, which faces Isla Mujeres rather than the open Caribbean. The water is shallower and gentler there, which is why families with small children often stay on that stretch.
