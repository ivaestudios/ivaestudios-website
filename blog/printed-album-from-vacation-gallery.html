Which 20 Photos to Print From 300

By Vianey Díaz · August 7, 2026 · 8 min read

Every gallery we deliver has the same afterlife. It gets opened the night it arrives, shared with the family group chat that week, and then it lives in a browser tab and a phone camera roll until it quietly stops being opened at all. Three hundred photographs is too many to look at and far too many to love. Twenty printed ones, in order, in a book you can hand to somebody, is a different object entirely. This is the method we use when clients ask us to help them cut a vacation gallery down to an album, and the practical detail on paper, sizes, labs and shipping that nobody tells you until you are already stuck.

Why families are printing again

This is not nostalgia talking. The industry data points the same way. The Knot's 2026 trend reporting puts tangible keepsakes among the things couples are actively adding back, and Pinterest logged wedding projector searches up 625 percent, which is the same instinct pointed at a wall: people want the images to exist somewhere other than a feed. Photo books, framed prints and albums have been climbing for several years while the number of photographs taken per family has exploded. Those two facts are related. The more images there are, the less any single one is worth, and the more a printed one stands out.

There is a practical argument too, and it is the one we make more often. Digital delivery is fragile in ways people do not think about until it fails. Galleries expire, cloud accounts get abandoned, hard drives fail without warning and phones get replaced. A printed book fails only if the house burns down. We deliver every session in a private 4K gallery within one to three business days and the full-resolution files are yours to keep, but files are a raw material, not a finished object. The album is the finished object.

Selecting 20 from 300: the method we use

The reason people stall is that they try to do it in one pass, judging every image against every other image at once. Do it in three passes instead, and do the passes on different days if you can.

Pass one, thirty seconds per photo, gut only. Go through the whole gallery once and mark anything that makes you feel something. Do not think about duplicates, do not think about whether it will work in a book, do not think about whether your hair looks right. You are looking for reaction, not quality. Most people come out of this with sixty to ninety images from three hundred. That is the correct number, and if you have marked forty you were being too strict.

Pass two, kill the near-duplicates. Now group what survived. Almost any session produces clusters of five or six frames from the same setup, and only one belongs in a book. Pick by faces, in this order: everyone's eyes open, then the person who is hardest to photograph looking good, then the most natural expression on the smallest child. Ignore the background, the pose and the light, because they are nearly identical inside a cluster anyway. This normally halves the pile.

Pass three, build the cast list. Twenty photographs of a family trip should contain a specific set of things, and this is where you select against a checklist rather than against your feelings. You want at least one wide frame that establishes where you were, one full family group, one image of each individual person alone, one of each pairing that matters to you, at least two frames with no faces at all, and two or three that are messy and unposed. If a category is empty, go back to the gallery and find one even if it was not on your favourites list. If a category has six, cut it to two.

The rule that decides ties

When two images are genuinely equal, keep the one that tells you something you would otherwise forget. The exact height of your daughter next to her cousin. The sunburn. The hotel bracelet on a wrist. Perfection is not memorable. Detail is.

Sequencing an album so it reads as a trip

Twenty good photographs arranged badly is a grid. Twenty good photographs arranged well is a story, and the difference costs nothing.

Open wide. The first spread should place the viewer somewhere: the shoreline, the resort, the horizon, the light. Nobody's face yet. Then move inward, from the place to the group to the individuals. Give your strongest single image a spread of its own with nothing opposite it, because an image with white space around it reads as important and an image in a grid of four reads as filler. Alternate rhythm deliberately, a busy spread then a calm one, faces then no faces, so the eye gets somewhere to rest.

Two technical things that people get wrong. First, never put two portraits of different people facing away from each other across a gutter, because the spread pulls apart visually. Face them inward. Second, watch what falls into the fold. On a standard bound album the gutter eats several millimetres, so a face or a horizon placed dead centre across a spread will be damaged. Layflat binding solves this and is the main reason it costs more.

Close quiet. The last image should be the one that feels like leaving: backs to the camera, hands held, the sun already gone. Everybody knows this instinctively when they see it and almost nobody does it on the first attempt.

Sizes, paper and cover choices explained

Size first, because it drives everything else. For twenty images, a 20 by 20 centimetre or 25 by 25 centimetre square book is the sweet spot. Square handles both horizontal and vertical images without awkward cropping, and it sits on a coffee table properly. Anything smaller than 20 centimetres turns a family group into a row of unrecognisable heads. Anything above 30 centimetres starts to need more than twenty photographs to feel filled, and the price climbs steeply.

Binding is the choice that matters most. A layflat album, where each page is a rigid spread that opens completely flat with no visible gutter, is what a professional wedding album is. A standard perfect-bound photo book, the kind every consumer lab prints, curves into the fold. Layflat is roughly three to five times the price. For a vacation album we tell people honestly: if there are two or three images you want to run across a full spread, pay for layflat. If every image sits on its own page, a good perfect-bound book is fine and the money is better spent on a larger size.

Paper. Lustre is the safe default, it holds saturation, resists fingerprints and does not glare under a lamp. Matte is beautiful for skin tones and is what we prefer for beach work, but it shows handling marks over years. Glossy photographic paper is the one to avoid in a book, because every spread mirrors the ceiling light. Metallic papers oversaturate skin and turn a warm golden hour frame orange. For loose prints going behind glass in a frame, matte is the right call, because glossy behind glass produces a double reflection.

Covers. Linen ages best and still looks right in fifteen years, particularly with debossed names and the year. A photographic cover, where an image wraps the board, dates faster, so if you choose one use a wide landscape frame rather than faces.

Printing in Mexico versus shipping to the US or Canada

This is where most plans get complicated, so here is the straightforward version. Your files are digital. There is no reason the printing has to happen in the same country as the photography.

Printing locally in Mexico makes sense in one case: you are still in the country, you want prints in your hands before you fly home, and you are happy with a same-week turnaround from a lab in Cancún, Mérida or Mexico City. Loose prints in that scenario are fast and cheap. A bound album is harder, because the good Mexican book labs work on a one to three week schedule and you will already have left.

Ordering from a lab in your own country is what we recommend for almost everyone. You upload the files, the book prints and ships domestically, there is no customs step, and if the colour comes back wrong you can reprint without an international return. Shipping a finished book from Mexico to the United States or Canada is possible but it adds a week or more, it can attract a customs charge, and a damaged-in-transit album is a genuinely painful thing to sort out from another country.

One technical note that saves grief. Our galleries deliver full resolution files in sRGB, which is what every consumer and most professional labs expect. Before you commit to a twenty page book, order a single 20 by 25 centimetre test print from that specific lab of an image with faces in it. If it comes back too dark or too magenta, that is the lab's profile, not your file, and you have found out for the price of one print rather than a whole album.

Costs and timelines to expect

Prices move and vary enormously by country, so treat these as the shape of the market rather than quotes. A consumer photo book from a mainstream online lab, 20 to 25 centimetres square, twenty to thirty pages, perfect bound, lands roughly in the 40 to 120 US dollar range. A professional layflat album at the same size, printed on thick photographic stock with a linen cover, runs from around 250 to 700 US dollars depending on page count and cover. Individual prints are trivial by comparison, a few dollars each, more for fine-art cotton rag.

Timelines. Consumer labs typically produce in three to seven business days plus shipping. Professional album houses run two to four weeks, longer between October and December when every wedding photographer in the northern hemisphere is placing orders at once. If the album is a Christmas gift, place the order in early November at the latest.

The honest budgeting advice: the biggest quality jump is not layflat binding or fine-art paper, it is cutting from forty images to twenty.

"Three hundred photographs is an archive. Twenty is a story. Only one of the two ever gets looked at twice."

Give the grandparents the album, not the link

This is the part clients thank us for later. A gallery link sent to a grandparent is, in practice, a gift that requires them to have a device, a password, a working browser and the patience to scroll. A book requires none of that, and it sits somewhere visible in their house for the rest of their life.

Two versions is the pattern that works. One larger album for you, and a small, cheap, twenty page book for each set of grandparents, printed from the same twenty images. The second and third copies of an identical book usually cost a fraction of the first because the layout already exists. It is the least expensive meaningful gift we know of, and every year we hear about the same thing happening: the book stays on the coffee table and the trip stays alive in a way the group chat never managed.

Questions about prints and albums

Are the files in my IVAE gallery big enough to print an album?

Yes. Sessions are delivered at full resolution in a private 4K gallery in sRGB, which is what consumer and professional labs expect. That is comfortably enough for a 20 or 25 centimetre square album and for standard framed prints. Before ordering a full book, order one test print of an image with faces from the specific lab you plan to use.

How many photos should actually go in a vacation album?

For a single session, twenty is the number we recommend, spread across roughly twenty to thirty pages. Twenty is enough to cover the wide establishing frames, the group, each individual, the pairings and a few unposed moments, and few enough that every page earns its place. Books above about forty images from one session start to feel repetitive.

Should I print in Mexico or at home?

For loose prints while you are still in the country, a local lab in Cancun or Merida is fast and inexpensive. For a bound album, ordering from a lab in your own country is almost always better: no customs step, domestic shipping, and easy reprints if the colour comes back wrong. The files are digital, so the printing does not have to happen where the photos were taken.

What is the difference between layflat and a normal photo book?

In a layflat album each page is a rigid spread that opens completely flat, so an image can run across two pages with no gutter and nothing is lost in the fold. A normal perfect-bound photo book curves into the centre. Layflat costs roughly three to five times more. If you want two or three full-spread images, it is worth it. If every image sits on its own page, it is not.

Which paper is best for beach and golden hour photos?

Lustre for a book, because it holds colour, resists fingerprints and does not mirror the ceiling light on every spread. Matte is lovely for skin tones and is our preference for beach work, with the caveat that it shows handling over the years. Avoid glossy in a bound book and avoid metallic papers entirely, because they push warm golden hour skin toward orange.

When should I order if the album is a gift?

Consumer labs produce in three to seven business days plus shipping, professional album houses in two to four weeks, and both slow down considerably from October through December. For a Christmas gift, place the order by early November. For an anniversary, give yourself a month, which also leaves room to reprint if a test print reveals a colour problem.
