In short

Yes. The ZOFEMAT federal strip, generally twenty metres inland from the high-tide line, is national property no hotel can own, and anyone may walk, sit, swim and take photographs there. The catch is access: a hotel controls its own entrances, so you reach the sand through a municipal access point, not a private lobby.

The resort quoted an outside vendor fee. Someone told you Mexican beaches are public. Now you are wondering whether you can simply walk twenty steps down the sand, stand outside the hotel's little roped area, and have your photos taken for free. It is the most common follow-up question we get, and the internet answers it badly in both directions. Here is what the law actually says, what it means in practice at a Cancún resort, and the honest calculation about whether the workaround is worth it.

What ZOFEMAT Is and Why the Sand Is Federal

ZOFEMAT stands for Zona Federal Marítimo Terrestre, the federal maritime land zone. It is a strip of land, generally twenty metres wide, measured horizontally inland from the highest tide line along the whole Mexican coast. That strip is national property under the Ley General de Bienes Nacionales, and it cannot be sold. No hotel owns it. No private residence owns it. No beach club owns it.

The zone is administered federally through SEMARNAT, and its day to day management is usually delegated to the municipality, which is why in Benito Juárez, the municipality that contains Cancún, you will hear people talk about the municipal ZOFEMAT office. Hotels and beach clubs frequently hold concessions over parts of the zone, which lets them place loungers, palapas, walkways and service structures on the sand. A concession is a permit to use, not a title of ownership, and it never extinguishes the public character of the strip.

The twenty metres also moves. It is measured from the tide line, so on a wide beach after a good sand replenishment season the public strip sits well away from the hotel, and on an eroded stretch after a hurricane season it can arrive at the hotel's own wall. That variability is why the answer to "where exactly can I stand" is genuinely fuzzy on some beaches and obvious on others.

The distinction that explains everything

The sand is public. The access is not. Mexican law guarantees that beaches belong to the nation, but it does not require a private hotel to let strangers walk through its lobby and pool deck to reach them. Public beach plus private access equals a beach that is legally yours and practically theirs, unless you enter from a municipal access point.

What the Public Actually Has a Right to Do

Anyone can walk, sit, swim, sunbathe and take photographs on the federal strip. That includes a bride and groom. It includes a photographer. There is no licence for holding a camera on a Mexican beach, and a couple with a photographer at golden hour is doing something that thousands of visitors do daily without a thought.

What the public does not automatically get is the right to occupy the sand with a setup. Placing chairs, building an arch, running a generator, erecting lighting stands, roping off an area, bringing a sound system, or gathering a large group for a staged event all cross from use into occupation, and that is when the municipal ZOFEMAT office becomes relevant and an authorization can be required. A ceremony with seated guests on a public beach is not the same activity as two people photographed walking at the waterline, even though both technically happen on the same sand.

Where the Resort's Authority Begins

A hotel's authority is real, it is just narrower than the front desk implies. Everything above the federal strip is unambiguously theirs: the rooms, the corridors, the lobby, the gardens, the pool deck, the restaurants, the gazebo, the lawn. On the sand itself, their concession covers the furniture and structures they placed there, which means their loungers, their palapas, their beach club deck, their walkways and their service areas.

So the enforcement picture looks like this. If you are photographed in the room getting ready, on the terrace, at the pool, on the beach club deck or sitting on their loungers, you are within their operation and the outside vendor fee applies to your photographer. If you are walking along the open waterline, past the last row of their loungers, you are on federal land and no vendor fee is legally triggered.

"The sand belongs to the nation. The chairs belong to the hotel. Almost every dispute on a Mexican beach comes down to which one you sat on."

Can You Walk Off-Property to Dodge the Fee? Honestly

Yes, and it works less often than couples imagine. Here is the honest accounting.

The workaround genuinely works when the session is a portrait session and nothing else. An engagement shoot, an anniversary session, a trash-the-dress the day after, a honeymoon couples session: all of these can start and finish on a public beach with no resort involvement whatsoever. You meet us at a municipal access point, we shoot the golden hour on federal sand, and no fee exists to pay. We do this every week and the galleries are indistinguishable from resort work, because the ocean does not know where the property line is.

The workaround does not work for a wedding day. A wedding is not a session. It is getting ready in a room, a first look in a corridor, a ceremony on their lawn or their beach platform, family formals near the gazebo, a cocktail hour on their terrace and a reception in their ballroom. Almost all of that happens on private property. Walking outside for twenty minutes of portraits does not remove the photographer from the property for the other seven hours, and no serious resort will pretend otherwise. Trying to argue it usually costs you goodwill with the coordinator whose help you will need all day.

There is also a middle case worth naming. Some couples staying at a property with a high vendor fee choose to book a short, separate off-property session on a different day, usually the morning after, and keep the wedding-day coverage minimal. That is a legitimate way to get more images for less money, and we say more about how it works on our Cancún wedding photographer page.

Public Access Points in the Hotel Zone That Work

Cancún's Hotel Zone is a narrow barrier island with a single boulevard running its length, and the municipality maintains public beach access points along it. These are the doors that make the public beach actually public, and knowing them is the whole trick.

  • Playa Delfines, roughly km 18. The largest public beach in the Hotel Zone, with a big parking area, a raised dune viewpoint and the CANCÚN letters. No hotel sits directly on it, which is exactly why it is the safest place to shoot without any conversation.
  • Playa Marlin, roughly km 13. A municipal access beside a shopping centre, with parking, showers and lifeguards. Wide, clean sand and open Caribbean.
  • Playa Chac Mool, roughly km 10. Municipal access on the calmer stretch, popular with locals in the evening, with restaurants right behind it.
  • Playa Caracol and Playa Tortugas, roughly km 8.5 and km 6.5. Bay side, calm shallow water, sheltered from wind. Better for family and children's sessions than for dramatic wedding portraits, and useful on days when the open coast is rough.

that beaches on the open Caribbean side receive sargassum in variable amounts between roughly April and October, which is the single biggest thing that changes how a public beach looks in a photograph.

Playa Delfines, Marlin and Chac Mool: Crowds and Light by Hour

A public beach is a working environment, and the difference between a good frame and a crowded one is almost entirely the hour you pick.

Playa Delfines faces east onto the open Caribbean and it is the most dramatic of the three, with a high dune that lets us shoot down onto the sand and a genuinely uninterrupted horizon. It fills from mid-morning and is at its worst around midday, both for crowds and for the flat overhead light. Sunrise, roughly the first hour after first light, is extraordinary there and almost empty. The last forty minutes before sunset are warm and busy, with the crowd concentrated near the letters and the parking, so we walk south along the sand and lose most of it.

Playa Marlin is smaller and reads more intimate. Its sand is wide and clean, the strip in front of the access is busy in the afternoon, and a five minute walk in either direction thins it out considerably. It works well in the last hour of light because the buildings behind it are set back enough not to intrude on a low horizontal frame.

Playa Chac Mool sits at a bend where the water is calmer and the sand slopes gently. It is popular with local families in the early evening, which is charming in real life and busy on camera. We use it mostly at sunrise, or on days when wind on the open coast makes veils and dresses unmanageable.

All three share the same enemy. Cancún sun between roughly eleven and three is harsh, high and unforgiving. No public beach looks good in it. We do not schedule sessions in that window on any beach, public or private, which is the reason the studio shoots golden hour only.

When We Recommend Paying the Fee Anyway

We are an independent studio. The vendor fee is the single biggest obstacle between us and a booking, so nobody has more incentive than we do to tell you it can be avoided. And still, on a wedding day, we usually recommend paying it. Here is the reasoning.

First, access buys the story. The morning in the room, the dress on the hanger, the mother's hands on the buttons, the walk down the corridor: those frames are half the emotional weight of the gallery and every one of them happens on private property. Second, the coordinator relationship is worth real money on a wedding day. A photographer the resort recognizes as authorized can move through service corridors, get onto a balcony for the wide ceremony frame, and hold a spot at the gazebo. A photographer they consider unauthorized spends the day being managed. Third, the fee frequently comes with the ceremony location itself, which is the one part of the day that cannot happen twice.

Where we do push back is when a resort quotes a fee that is out of proportion and refuses to explain what it covers. It is a fair question to ask what the fee includes, whether it changes if we hold a day pass, and whether the resort's in-house team is being protected rather than the property. Some properties are transparent and reasonable. Some are not, and knowing which is which before you book the hotel is worth more than any negotiation afterwards. Our venue directory and our destination wedding guide note where those fees tend to land.

For everything that is not a wedding day, use the public beach without hesitation. It is yours. The studio is based in Cancún, shoots the whole coast every week, and if you want to see what an entirely off-property session looks like, our Cancún photographer page is built almost entirely from public sand.

One caution before you plan around this

Federal law sets the framework, but municipalities administer the zone and resorts write their own contracts. Rules on setups, access hours and drone use vary by municipality and change with local administrations. Nothing here is legal advice. If your plan involves anything larger than a couple and a camera, ask the municipal ZOFEMAT office or your wedding planner in writing well ahead of the date.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are all beaches in Mexico public?
Yes. Under Mexican federal law the sand itself is national property, and no hotel, resort or private owner can own a beach. What the law protects is the strip of sand, generally the twenty metres measured inland from the high-tide line, known as the Zona Federal Marítimo Terrestre or ZOFEMAT. Being public does not mean it is freely reachable, because the land behind the beach can be private, and that is where most of the confusion starts.
Can I take wedding photos on a public beach in Cancun without a permit?
For a couple and a photographer walking the sand at golden hour, in practice yes. No permit exists for a small portrait session on a public beach, and nobody is going to stop two people and a camera. What changes the situation is scale and staging: chairs, arches, a large group, lighting stands, generators or anything that occupies the sand as a setup can require authorization from the municipal ZOFEMAT office, and a hotel's concession staff will intervene if you use their furniture or their access.
If the beach is public, can we skip the resort's outside vendor fee?
Sometimes, and it is more limited than couples hope. If you are getting ready in a resort room, being photographed in the lobby, at the pool or on the resort's own beach furniture, you are on their property and their fee applies. If you leave the property entirely, enter the beach through a public access point and photograph on the open sand, you are on federal land and the resort has no claim over you. The trade is real, though: you lose the getting-ready coverage, the resort backdrop and the convenience, and you gain a walk in a gown.
Which public beaches in Cancun are best for photos?
Playa Delfines at kilometre 18 is the largest and most photogenic, with a raised dune that gives elevated angles and the big CANCÚN letters. Playa Marlin near kilometre 13 is smaller, calmer and has a wide clean strip of sand. Playa Chac Mool at kilometre 10 sits on the bay side with softer water. All three have municipal access, parking and lifeguards, and all three are busiest between eleven and four. We shoot them at first light or the last hour before sunset.
Will a resort stop us from photographing on the sand in front of their hotel?
They can ask you to leave their loungers, their beach club area, their palapas and their walkways, and they are entitled to do that because those are concessioned or private. They cannot legally close the sand strip to a person walking along it. In practice the interaction is polite and the answer is to keep moving along the waterline rather than argue. We plan routes that never require that conversation.
Do you charge extra to shoot off-property instead of at the resort?
No. Our pricing is based on hours and locations, not on which side of a property line we stand on. Where an outside cost exists, such as a resort vendor fee, a beach club consumption minimum or a cenote entrance, we itemize it in writing before you book so there are no surprises. Every gallery is delivered in one to three business days in a private 4K gallery regardless of where we shoot.
