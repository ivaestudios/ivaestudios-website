Film ranks first on The WED's 2026 trend list, roll volumes have climbed steadily since 2020, and we now get asked about it in roughly one inquiry in five. We like film. We also think couples deserve a straight answer about what shooting it at a destination wedding in Mexico involves, because the honest version is not the version that gets posted. Heat, humidity, airport scanners, no local lab, no reshoot, and thirty-six frames to cover a day that will never happen again. Here are those constraints, and what we do instead.

Why Film Came Back, and What It Does to a Frame

Colour negative film holds highlights the way digital does not. Point a sensor at a white dress in Caribbean sun and there is a hard cliff: below it detail, above it nothing. Negative film rolls off instead, so the brightest part of a dress compresses gently rather than disappearing. On a beach at 21 degrees north with white sand, white linen and a white dress, that is not a subtle advantage.

Film also has grain rather than noise. Grain is structural and sits in the image the way texture sits in paper, while digital noise is an artefact and reads as an error. And film has halation, the soft red bloom around a bright light source caused by light passing through the emulsion and reflecting back. That glow around a string light or a low sun is one of the most recognisable film signatures and the hardest to manufacture convincingly.

Last, film changes the photographer. Thirty-six frames per roll means you wait. You do not spray a moment, you watch it and take one.

Heat and Humidity: What Happens in Quintana Roo

Colour film is a chemical product with a comfort range and Quintana Roo sits outside it most of the year. Manufacturers generally want unexposed colour film held below about 24 degrees Celsius, roughly 75 Fahrenheit. Cancún daytime highs run 28 to 33 through most of the calendar and humidity sits above 80 percent for long stretches of the wet season, roughly June through October. Sustained heat raises base fog and shifts colour balance, worst on higher-speed stocks and on slide film. The effect is cumulative, so a roll in a hot car for six hours is a real problem and a roll in a shoulder bag for forty minutes is not.

The routine is simple and non-negotiable. Film travels sealed, stays in a fridge or cooler between uses, and never sits in a vehicle. The counterintuitive part is taking it out: cold canisters brought straight into 30 degree air at 85 percent humidity attract condensation, and condensation on an emulsion is worse than warmth, so sealed film comes out of the fridge at least an hour before it is opened. Exposed film is more fragile still, and should go back into the cold immediately and reach a lab fast, which on this coast is not possible at all.

The rule we tell couples first

Film never goes in checked baggage. Checked-bag scanners are far more powerful than carry-on machines and will fog an emulsion in a single pass. Lead-lined bags do not solve this, because operators simply increase the dose until they can see through them.

Airport Scanners at CUN and TQO

This is the part that has changed most recently, and the advice couples find online is often five years out of date.

The old carry-on X-ray machines were survivable, and the guidance was simply to limit the passes. That no longer holds. The newer computed tomography scanners now installed at many major airports rotate a much stronger beam around the bag to build a three-dimensional image, and they damage film at any speed, with a single pass sometimes enough to matter. Lead-lined bags do not help, because operators increase the dose until they can see through them.

Cancún International is one of the busiest airports in Mexico and Tulum's Felipe Carrillo Puerto International opened in December 2023, and equipment at both is upgraded on the operators' own schedule, so nobody can promise in advance what you will meet at security. The only reliable protection is a hand inspection, requested politely with the film out of its boxes and tubs so the officer can see it quickly. That is a request, not a right, so plan for being refused, because if you are you have a decision to make on the spot with your wedding film in your hand.

Developing: Where the Labs Are and How Long It Takes

There is no professional colour lab in Cancún, Playa del Carmen or Tulum that we would send a wedding to. Processing with proper scanning is concentrated in Mexico City and the United States, which means exposed film travels again, either in a carry-on for a second scanner encounter or by courier, with its own heat exposure in a cargo hold.

Turnaround is the other half. Between shipping, the lab queue, developing, scanning and return, two to five weeks is normal and it stretches around holidays. Set that beside a digital gallery that lands in 1 to 3 business days. It is not a reason not to shoot film, it is a reason to be clear that the film set is a second, later delivery and not the record of your wedding.

"The question is not whether film is beautiful. It is whether you want the only copy of a moment to be travelling through a scanner in someone's carry-on three days later."

Cost and Risk of Adding a Roll

Cost is the easy part. Film, developing and scanning commonly land around forty to seventy US dollars per 35mm roll in 2026, more for medium format. Against the total of a destination wedding that is not a serious number.

Risk is the real number and it has no price. A single roll is thirty-six frames total, not thirty-six keepers, and medium format gives you sixteen or fewer. Across an eight hour wedding day a roll is a handful of moments.

Every failure mode is total. A light leak in a hot camera back, a roll that never caught the take-up spool, a mislabelled canister, a lab accident, a scanner in an airport. When film fails it does not degrade, it disappears, and there is no wedding day to go back to. Digital fails differently: a card can be recovered, a second body is already recording, and we carry duplicates of everything. That asymmetry is the whole argument.

Hybrid Coverage: Digital Primary, Film Accents

So here is how we handle a couple who genuinely wants real film at a destination wedding in Mexico. Digital is the record: every moment covered by the Director, on two bodies, with duplicate cards.

Film is an accent, scoped to two windows: getting ready, where the light is soft and the pace allows deliberation, and the golden hour portrait block, which is exactly the light film was made for. That is one or two rolls across a day, not coverage.

The film set is a bonus, delivered separately when the lab returns it, with an honest warning attached: some of it may not come back. Couples who understand that in advance are almost always delighted with what does. Couples who were promised film as a guaranteed deliverable are the ones who get hurt.

Getting the Film Look in Camera Instead of in a Preset

When we ask couples what they love about the film photos they have saved, the answers are almost never chemical. They say warm, soft, real, not overdone. All of that is achievable in camera, and it is what we do at every wedding whether or not film is involved.

It starts with the hour. We only shoot in the golden hour, so the light is already low, warm and directional before any decision is made about equipment, and that single scheduling choice does more for the look than any stock or preset. Then exposure: we place highlights deliberately instead of letting a meter clip them, and let shadows sit lower instead of lifting everything into flat grey, which is negative film's tonal logic applied to a sensor. Then glass, because lenses that flare and render with lower micro-contrast do a lot of the work, especially into low sun or sea haze. Then the edit: grain stays, skin is not smoothed, and greens stay slightly desaturated rather than pushed toward turquoise, the most common way a Caribbean gallery ends up looking synthetic.

What this cannot fully replicate is halation and the way a negative handles a completely blown highlight. If those are what you are chasing, you want real film and you should accept the constraints above with open eyes. If what you are chasing is warmth, texture and restraint, you can have it in your gallery in 1 to 3 business days.

Common Questions About This

Do you shoot film at destination weddings in Mexico?

Only as an accent, and only when a couple asks for it and understands the trade. Digital is always the primary record. If film is part of the plan we agree in advance which moments get a roll, usually getting ready and the golden hour portraits, and we treat those frames as a bonus set that arrives later, not as part of your delivered gallery.

Will my film photos arrive with my digital gallery?

No, and this is the part couples underestimate. Your digital gallery arrives in 1 to 3 business days at 4K. Film has to be shipped to a professional lab, developed, scanned and returned, which realistically is two to five weeks from the wedding day and longer around holidays. Plan for the film set as a second delivery.

Can film survive the flight and the heat in Cancun?

It can, with handling. Film never goes in checked baggage, because those scanners are far more powerful than carry-on machines and will fog it. In carry-on, the newer CT scanners now common at major airports damage film at any speed, so the only reliable answer is a hand inspection request, which staff may or may not grant. On the ground it stays cool and sealed, and needs an hour at room temperature before the canister is opened so condensation does not form on the emulsion.

Is film worth the extra cost at a destination wedding?

It depends why you want it. If you want a small number of physical, imperfect, genuinely analogue frames as an object, yes, and the cost is modest against a destination wedding budget. If what you want is warmth, grain and softer highlights, we can get most of the way there in camera with none of the risk. We will ask which one you mean before anything is booked.

Can you get the film look without shooting film?

A long way, yes. Most of what people identify as the film look is not chemistry, it is light and restraint: shooting in the golden hour, exposing so highlights roll off instead of clipping, gentle contrast, grain left in, skin not smoothed, and lenses that flare rather than resist. What a preset cannot convincingly fake is halation and the way a negative handles a completely blown highlight. Everything else is a shooting decision.

Ask one question before you commit: do you want film as an object, a small set of physical, imperfect, genuinely analogue frames you will keep in a box, or do you want the way film looks? Those are different purchases. The first is worth the risk and the wait. The second carries neither.
