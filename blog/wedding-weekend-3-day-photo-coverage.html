3-Day Wedding Weekend Photo Coverage Plan

A destination wedding stopped being a single day some time ago. Zola's planning data has around 37 percent of couples now hosting additional events beyond the ceremony and reception, and roughly 18 percent building a full two to three day weekend. The Knot's 2026 photography trends put coverage of welcome dinners and recovery brunches on the list explicitly. If your guests are flying to Mexico, they are not coming for four hours, and neither should your photographer. Here is how we actually plan a three day weekend, hour by hour, with honest numbers.

Why Multi-Day Is Now the Default at Mexican Resorts

Two things drove this, and neither is fashion. The first is travel maths. If a guest spends a day getting to Cancún and a day getting home, a single four hour event is a poor return on their trip, and couples feel that. The second is the all-inclusive structure itself. When everyone is already staying at the same property with food and drink included, adding a Friday welcome party and a Sunday brunch costs a fraction of what those events would cost at home, and frequently the resort's wedding package includes at least one of them.

The result is a weekend that has its own shape. Friday is arrival and reunion. Saturday is the wedding. Sunday is the long goodbye. Each has a completely different emotional temperature, and photographically each behaves differently. Treating the weekend as one long wedding day with gaps in it is the mistake we see most often in coverage plans written by people who have not worked one.

The one thing to decide first

Before you allocate a single hour, decide which day is the emotional centre of your weekend. For most couples it is Saturday. For some, particularly couples who married legally at home, it is the Friday welcome party where everyone finally sees each other. That answer changes where the coverage weight goes, and it is worth saying out loud early.

Friday: Arrivals, Welcome Party and the First Golden Hour

Friday is the most underrated day of a wedding weekend, and it is the one couples most often skip covering. That is a mistake, because Friday hands you something Saturday cannot: everyone in the same place with no pressure on them.

The welcome party itself is where the value is. Two hours covering it, ideally starting forty five minutes before sunset, gives you three distinct things. First, arrival and greeting frames, which are the most genuinely emotional images of the whole weekend because the reunions are real and unrehearsed. Second, relaxed group portraits, taken in golden light with guests in linen and sundresses rather than formalwear, which many couples end up preferring to the stiff Saturday formals. Third, the couple together, calm, the night before, which is a portrait that only exists on Friday.

A practical note on scheduling. At a resort welcome party, ask your coordinator to set the start time by sunset rather than by a round number. A 6:00 PM start in December and a 6:00 PM start in June are entirely different light. We work the sunset time for your specific date backwards and hand the coordinator a start time that puts the first drinks in warm light instead of blue dusk.

Saturday: The Wedding Day, Hour by Hour

Saturday is the day the whole weekend is built around, and it is the one place we do not recommend economising. Here is the structure we build for a typical Riviera Maya or Cancún resort wedding, working backwards from sunset. Assume a 6:30 PM sunset, which is roughly right for spring and autumn on this coast, and shift the whole thing with your date.

  • 1:30 PM, details and getting ready begins. Dress, rings, shoes, invitation suite, flowers, shot in the best window light in the room. This is quiet work and it needs about forty five minutes without people moving through.
  • 2:15 PM, hair and makeup finals, both parties. This is where a second shooter earns its money, because the two suites are frequently on opposite sides of a large resort and one photographer physically cannot cover both.
  • 3:30 PM, into the dress, first look with a parent. The most emotional twenty minutes of the day and the most commonly under-scheduled.
  • 4:00 PM, couple's first look and immediate portraits. Optional, but it moves a large block of portraits off the post-ceremony window and gives you back the cocktail hour.
  • 5:00 PM, ceremony. On a beach, this timing puts the light behind and above the aisle without hard shadow on faces. Ninety minutes before sunset is our standard target.
  • 5:35 PM, family formals. Twenty five minutes, list prepared in advance, one person from each family designated to call names. Longer than that and you lose the light.
  • 6:05 PM, couple's golden hour. Twenty five minutes, just the two of you, walking. Non-negotiable in our plans. This is where the images that end up on the wall come from.
  • 6:45 PM, reception entrance, dinner, speeches. Blue hour lands here and the venue lighting takes over.
  • 9:00 PM onward, first dance, party, sparkler or night portrait. Coverage typically ends between ten and eleven, after the dance floor is genuinely going.

That is eight to ten hours depending on where you start and stop. Ten covers everything from the dress on the hanger to a full dance floor. Eight covers everything except the earliest details or the last hour of the party. Below eight, something meaningful gets cut, and it is usually either the getting ready or the party.

"Set the ceremony time from the sunset time, then build everything else backwards. Every wedding weekend that goes wrong photographically went wrong at that one decision."

Sunday: Recovery Brunch and the Day-After Session

Sunday is the day couples forget to plan and then remember fondly. The recovery brunch is a genuinely lovely event to photograph precisely because it is the opposite of the wedding: nobody is dressed up, everybody is comparing notes on the night before, and the couple finally gets to sit down with people.

Two hours covers a brunch comfortably. Realistic timing is between ten and noon, late enough that people have slept and early enough to clear the resort's checkout. Expect the first thirty minutes to be thin and the middle hour to be dense, as tables fill and people rotate.

The day-after portrait session is the other half of Sunday and it is the best value in our entire offering. One to two hours, back in the dress or in something lighter, at a location the wedding day had no time for. On this coast that usually means a cenote, a quiet stretch of beach north or south of the resort, or a jungle road. There is no timeline pressure, no coordinator, nobody waiting, and the couple has completely relaxed. The pictures show it.

Schedule the portraits before the brunch, not after. Sunrise or the two hours after it gives you the softest light of the entire weekend and an empty beach. If you push portraits to after the brunch you land in midday sun, which on this coast is the worst light available. We write more about that specific session in our guide to the recovery brunch and day-after session.

How Many Hours Each Day Realistically Needs

Here is the plain arithmetic we give couples, with the honest note on what each cut actually removes.

  • Friday, two hours. Welcome party from forty five minutes before sunset. Three hours if arrivals matter to you or the party has a structured element like a rehearsal or a family blessing.
  • Saturday, ten hours. Full day from details to dance floor. Eight hours is a reasonable trim and cuts either the early details or the last hour of the party. Six hours means you are choosing between getting ready and the reception, and we will tell you that plainly rather than let you find out afterwards.
  • Sunday, two to three hours. One to two for the day-after portraits at sunrise, one to two for the brunch. These can be booked independently.

Total: twelve to sixteen hours across three days. If budget forces a choice, our order of priority is Saturday first and by a distance, then the Sunday day-after portraits, then the Friday welcome party, then the Sunday brunch. That order surprises couples, because the brunch feels important while planning, but the day-after session produces far more images that end up printed.

One Photographer or Two: Where the Second Shooter Pays Off

A second shooter is not a general upgrade. It solves specific problems, and outside those problems it adds cost without adding much.

It pays for itself in exactly two windows. The first is getting ready, when two parties are preparing simultaneously in separate rooms, often several minutes apart across a large resort. One photographer covering both means rushing one and shortchanging the other, or building a timeline that forces everyone to finish at different times. The second is the ceremony, where a camera at the front and a camera at the back produce two angles of the same irreplaceable thirty seconds, and the reverse angle of the aisle with the couple's faces is impossible to get any other way.

For a weekend, our standard recommendation is a second shooter on Saturday only. Friday and Sunday are single-location, low-density events where one photographer moves faster and less visibly than two.

Building the Weekend Around Light Instead of Tradition

The studio shoots golden hour only, and on a three day weekend that principle becomes a planning tool rather than a slogan. Here is what it means concretely.

Each of your three days has two usable windows: the ninety minutes after sunrise and the ninety minutes before sunset. That is six windows across a weekend. Every genuinely important photographic moment should sit inside one of them, and everything else should be scheduled around whatever is left. Friday evening party inside the sunset window. Saturday ceremony and portraits inside the sunset window. Sunday portraits inside the sunrise window. Three of the six, used well, and the weekend's gallery is set.

What gets moved is tradition. A 2:00 PM ceremony because that is when weddings happen at home is the single most expensive decision you can make photographically on this coast, and it is entirely avoidable. Cancún and Riviera Maya sun between eleven and three is high, hard and unforgiving, and no amount of skill or retouching recovers it. Brunches, spa mornings, welcome lunches and travel all belong in that window. Ceremonies and portraits do not.

Sunset also moves substantially through the year here. A December wedding and a June wedding at the same resort need ceremony times more than an hour apart, and we calculate the exact figure for your date before we say anything about the schedule. Our destination wedding photography page explains how we build that timeline, and the Riviera Maya wedding photographer and Cancún wedding photographer pages cover the specific properties on each stretch of coast. If you are still choosing where to hold it, the venue directory notes which resorts have both a sunrise-facing beach and a sunset-facing lagoon, which is the ideal combination for a full weekend.

One last piece of honesty about multi-day coverage: it depends on your resort. Some properties treat the welcome party and the brunch as part of the wedding package with no extra vendor charge; others treat every event as a separate billable function with its own outside vendor fee. Ask that question in writing before you build a three day plan around it, because the answer varies wildly between properties on the same stretch of beach.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many hours of photography do we need for a three day wedding weekend?
As a working baseline, two to three hours on Friday for the welcome party, eight to ten hours on Saturday for the wedding day, and two to three hours on Sunday for the brunch and a day-after session. That lands at roughly twelve to sixteen hours across the weekend. The Saturday number is the one that should not be cut. Friday and Sunday are where couples can flex depending on budget and how much is actually happening.
Is a welcome dinner worth photographing?
At a destination wedding, more than at any other kind. The welcome party is the only moment where every guest who travelled is in one relaxed place, nobody is nervous yet, and the couple can actually talk to people. It is also the best source of candid group portraits, because guests are in resort clothes, the light is soft and there is no schedule pressure. Two hours of coverage there frequently produces more usable images per hour than the wedding day itself.
What is a day-after session?
A portrait session on the morning after the wedding, usually one to two hours, with the couple back in wedding clothes or in something lighter. It happens in fresh light at a location the wedding day did not allow time for, a cenote, a quiet beach, a jungle road. It is the single best value add-on we offer, because it doubles the portrait content of a gallery at a fraction of what an extra wedding-day hour costs.
Do we need a second photographer for the whole weekend?
Almost never. A second shooter earns its cost on the wedding day, specifically in the getting-ready window when two parties are preparing in separate rooms, and during the ceremony when a second angle from the back cannot be captured any other way. On Friday and Sunday the events are small, slow and single-location, and one photographer covers them comfortably. We usually recommend a second shooter for Saturday only.
When should we schedule the recovery brunch?
Late enough that people have slept and early enough to clear resort checkout, which usually means somewhere between ten and noon. If you want day-after portraits with good light, do those first at sunrise or shortly after and let the brunch follow. Trying to shoot portraits after a late brunch pushes you into flat midday sun, which is the one hour of the day we avoid on this coast.
How quickly will we see the photos from each day?
We deliver a private 4K gallery within one to three business days. On weekend coverage that usually means the Friday welcome party images are with the couple before the Sunday brunch, which guests love, and the full weekend gallery lands within days of everyone getting home. The turnaround is one of the reasons we keep the studio capped at twelve weddings a year.
