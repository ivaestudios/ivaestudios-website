Three portfolios, three words: fine art, editorial, lifestyle. They name different types of family photography, and the difference shows in the clothes you pack, the hour you book and the frame over your sofa. Here is what each looks like on a Cancun beach, from a family of four to sixteen people across three generations.

Fine Art, Editorial or Lifestyle: The Short Version

On a portfolio these words describe a look, not a qualification, so judge the pictures rather than the label; most sessions blend two. Documentary, a fourth style, observes without directing; see our guide to editorial vs documentary photography.

Fine Art Family Portraits: One Quiet Frame for the Wall

What is considered fine art photography? There is no official test, but museums offer a starting point. Tate Modern's exhibition Light and Magic: The Birth of Art Photography (14 October 2026 to 21 February 2027) traces art photography to pictorialism, which Tate calls the first international movement of its kind. From the 1880s, its exhibition guide explains, those photographers wanted images “as imaginative, expressive and technically considered as any painting or drawing”, and portraits with personality and a narrative.

Fine art family portraits make that ambition quieter: soft, even light, unhurried poses, muted color, clothes without logos or trends, calm space around the family. Fine art family portrait photography serves one purpose above all, a large print that still looks right in twenty years, so it favors a few strong, still frames over variety. Plan those while the youngest are fresh.

Editorial Family Photos: A Story Told by Clothes and Setting

An editorial family photoshoot is planned like a magazine spread. The wardrobe sets a palette, the setting matches it, and the session moves through scenes: everyone walking toward the water, the parents alone, the grandparents with the grandchildren, each child up close. The photographer directs where to stand, look and move; done well, the direction disappears and your family looks like itself, styled.

Editorial style family photos give you range: wide frames where the sea or the resort joins the story, close ones where a hand or a hem carries it. Marouchka G. came with a 1-year-old and a 4-year-old:

“They organized a last minute sunset shoot for our family with great art direction, and made it fun, short and efficient”

Marouchka G., Google review

Lifestyle Family Photos: Play, Movement, Real Laughs

Lifestyle sits at the other end. The photographer sets up something to do, a race to the water, a spin, the youngest on a parent's shoulders, and photographs what happens between prompts. It is the easiest look for toddlers and energetic children and the hardest to hang as a formal portrait, so pair it with one fine art or editorial frame. Adam H. described the balance:

“They were great with the kids, knew all the right spots and captured a lot of natural moments instead of overly posed shots.”

The Same Beach at Sunrise and at 5:00 pm

Online booking offers 6:30, 7:00 and 7:30 am and 5:00 and 5:30 pm; other times are arranged on WhatsApp. The hour changes the look as much as the clothes.

Around sunrise, the light is low, soft and slightly cool, and the water reads pale and quiet: the natural hour for fine art, and for pastel editorial palettes.

At 5:00 pm, the light warms as the sun drops; how close it runs to sunset depends on the month, and our sunset and golden hour table has your dates. Terracotta, olive and warm neutrals come alive and linen moves in a breeze: the classic hour for an editorial story or lifestyle play.

What to Wear for Each Look

  • Fine art: tonal and timeless. Ivory, cream, oatmeal or sand, or one soft color for everyone, in linen, gauze or a fine knit. Long hems, solid fabrics, no logos or busy prints.
  • Editorial: one palette, one idea. Cream with terracotta and rust for 5:00 pm, or ivory with dusty blue and one navy piece, plus a statement item such as a flowing dress or a linen suit.
  • Lifestyle: comfort first. Soft cotton they can run, sit and splash in, bare feet on the sand, and nothing anyone needs to keep adjusting.

Two looks come with The Story, The Editorial and The Heirloom, so you need not pick one style: wear the fine art palette for the wall portrait, then change for the rest. Before your session, we send guidance on what to wear:

Sixteen People, Three Generations

With 10 to 16 people, the full-group frame is the one every household wants to print, and the three styles share the work:

  • Fine art for the full group. One tonal palette makes sixteen people read as one family, and a calm, simple composition keeps every face clear.
  • Editorial for each branch. A scene for each household, the grandparents with all the grandchildren, the siblings together; our multigenerational family photo ideas list the frames.
  • Lifestyle for the cousins. A run to the water, a pile-up on the sand, the moment the grandparents laugh.

There is no limit on the number of people and no per-person charge. Four Questions to Ask Before You Book

Ask every photographer you consider; our own answers to the last three are included.

  1. Can we see one full gallery from a single family session? A highlight reel shows a handful of the best frames; a full gallery shows whether the look holds all session.
  2. How much will you direct us? Fine art needs patient posing, editorial a plan, lifestyle games. We direct editorially, and our guidance before the day covers the scenes and how the session will run.
  3. How do you edit color? The grade is often the biggest difference between portfolios: fine art is frequently muted and painterly, other work pushes saturation. We edit for true color, so the Caribbean stays the color it was that day, and a quieter frame comes from early light and a soft palette rather than a faded edit; more in why we do not fade the Caribbean.
  4. What do we receive, and can we print it large? A private 4K gallery with unlimited downloads and print rights: a preview within 24 hours, the full gallery in 1 to 3 business days. For sizing, see large family portrait prints.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is considered fine art photography?

There is no official test. Tate traces art photography to pictorialism, which its exhibition guide dates to the 1880s, when photographers set out to make images as considered as paintings. In family portraits, fine art usually means one quiet, timeless frame: soft light, simple clothes, muted color, printed large.

What is an editorial family photoshoot?

A session planned like a magazine spread. Wardrobe, setting and a sequence of scenes are decided before the day, and the photographer directs where to stand, look and move. You get more variety than with fine art, and the clothes and setting carry the story.

What is the difference between fine art, editorial and lifestyle family photos?

Fine art aims for one timeless frame: still, soft and muted. Editorial builds a styled story carried by wardrobe and setting. Lifestyle follows play and movement. Many sessions mix two; choose by the photo you want on your wall.

Which look works for a big group or three generations?

Usually all three: a fine art frame of the whole group for every household's wall, editorial scenes for each branch and a lifestyle moment with the cousins. There is no limit on the number of people and no per-person charge; for groups of up to 16 we can arrange a full van, priced on request.
