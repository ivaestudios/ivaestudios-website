Splash Photos: Ending a Family Session in the Water

In short

Yes, the kids can get wet. Save the water for the last minutes, after the dry portraits, with swimsuits or rash guards as a second look (The Story, The Editorial and The Heirloom include two). Ask a child what they want to do at the beach and the answer is never “stand still and smile.” They want the water. Most parents want that too, just not before the portrait they plan to frame. You do not have to choose. Here is the plan we suggest for family beach photos in the water: the dressed-up frames first, the splash last, and safety ahead of both.

Save the Water for the Last Minutes

Order matters most. Once a child is soaked, salty and sandy, the posing is over, and so is the outfit: wet linen clings, damp hair sticks to cheeks, and a tired toddler wants a towel, not another portrait. So the frames you care about most come first, while everyone is clean and fresh: the whole family, each parent with the kids, the siblings side by side. Then the kids get the signal they have been waiting for.

Keeping the water for the end does three things:

  • Nobody spends the session damp. A wet child in a sea breeze gets chilly fast, above all on a cool winter morning.
  • The outfits survive. If the dry look is going on the wall, it should not be the one that met a wave.
  • It ends on a high. The splash is the one part where nobody asks a child to stand still, and it shows in the photos. It also gives the kids something to look forward to.

Pack for the change

Kids can wear swimsuits under the first look, so the switch takes a minute behind a towel. A long-sleeved rash guard covers shoulders and arms on the smallest ones. Choose swimsuits in the soft colors of look one; neon and big logos pull the eye from faces. Pack one towel per child in a color that suits your palette, since it may appear in the last frames, plus dry clothes and a bag for wet ones. Parents need not change: rolled-up linen trousers or a sundress you do not mind wetting at the hem look natural next to kids in swimsuits.

Pick Calm Water and an Early Start

For splash photos, the water matters more than the light. Small children belong where the sea is gentle and shallow, and in Cancun that depends on your stretch of beach. Our guides to the safest Cancun beaches for family photos with kids and to the calm bay versus the open sea in the Hotel Zone show where little ones can stand in the water and where they should stay at the very edge.

Then the hour. Online you can book a 6:30, 7:00 or 7:30 am start, or 5:00 or 5:30 pm; other times go through WhatsApp. With young kids, we suggest the morning: they are fresh, the beach is usually at its quietest, and swimsuits have all day to dry. The late-afternoon starts suit older kids and families who nap after lunch; in the hottest months, see our heat plan for a Cancun family session.

Or the resort pool

If the waves are up or seaweed has washed in, a shallow resort pool can take the splash instead; our page on Cancun beach conditions today shows how to check the seaweed map, webcams and flags the night before. Pools belong to the hotel, so the resort decides whether and when we can photograph there. We check your hotel's rule for outside photographers before the session; some hotels charge a fee for one, which you pay to the hotel. Floats are fine as props, nothing more: the American Academy of Pediatrics warns that floaties, water wings and inflatable toys can deflate and do not prevent drowning.

Safety First: the Shoreline and the Flag

Splash photos are shoreline play, not a swim. The frames that work happen where the waves end: wet sand, foam over small feet, ankle-deep water, never swimming depth. Every small child needs an adult within arm's length the whole time, which the American Academy of Pediatrics calls touch supervision and recommends for young children around water (page last updated May 18, 2026). Agree beforehand which adult is watching which child.

Then read the flag. On Cancun's public beaches, city lifeguards set it, and a city press release of October 26, 2024, issued through the Civil Protection office of Benito Juarez, Cancun's municipality, defines the colors:

  • Green: safe to swim.
  • Yellow: caution.
  • Red: entering the sea is prohibited.

It also asks visitors to check the flags on arrival and follow the lifeguards. Their hours vary: 9:00 am to 5:00 pm in that release, and 7:00 am to 7:00 pm during the summer 2026 vacation season (release of August 8, 2026, a Word file in Spanish). Either way, a 6:30 am start can begin before anyone is on the tower, and an earlier release, from July 20, 2023, says lifeguards leave the red flag up when they close. So an early red flag may be last night's: read it as red anyway. We checked every source on this page on November 14, 2026.

Reading the flag on the day

Red: no water at all. The finale moves up the sand (a race, a cartwheel, a towel-wrapped hug) or to the pool, if allowed. Yellow or green: the shoreline only, hand in hand, ankle-deep for little ones. At a resort, go by the flag it posts, and ask if you are unsure. If the sea looks rough to you, trust that and keep everyone on the sand.

Want a real swim in the photos? That is a different session: our guide to a cenote family photoshoot with kids covers ages, depth and safety in fresh water.

What the Splash Looks Like

The splash works because nobody poses. You play, and we photograph what happens. A few ideas:

  • Walking hand in hand through the foam line, like the family at the top of this page on Isla Mujeres.
  • Racing a wave up the sand, then chasing it back out.
  • A parent swinging a small child over the foam, toes skimming the water.
  • Kicking water at each other, parents included.
  • The towel finale: everyone wrapped up, hair dripping, still laughing.

If a child would rather stay dry, that is fine: a toddler watching the waves from a parent's hip is a lovely frame too. And because the splash is all movement, it suits video: session videos start with the 30-second Editorial Reel at 295 USD.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can kids get wet during family photos?

Yes, in the last minutes, after the dry portraits, so outfits stay clean and nobody spends the session damp. Keep it at the shoreline, with an adult within arm's length of every small child, and follow the beach flag: red means no water.

Which collection works for a dry look and a swimsuit look?

Any collection with two looks: The Story (75 minutes, 50 edited photos, 545 USD), The Editorial (90 minutes, 70 photos, 645 USD) or The Heirloom (90 minutes, the complete edited gallery, 895 USD). With The Chapter (60 minutes, 35 photos, 1 look, 475 USD), the splash happens in the same outfits. Moving up costs only the difference.

What time is best for splash photos with kids in Cancun?

For young children we suggest a morning start, 6:30, 7:00 or 7:30 am, when they are fresh and the beach is usually at its quietest. The 5:00 and 5:30 pm starts suit older kids; other times can be requested on WhatsApp.

What do the beach flags mean in Cancun?

According to Cancun's municipal Civil Protection office, green means safe to swim, yellow means caution and red means entering the sea is prohibited. Lifeguards leave the red flag up when they close, a 2023 city release says, so an early-morning red may be last night's. Treat it as red anyway.

Can we do the splash in the resort pool instead?

It depends on your resort, since pool areas follow the hotel's rules. We check your hotel's rule for outside photographers before the session; some hotels charge a fee for one, which you pay to the hotel. A shallow pool is a good plan B when the sea is rough.

When do we get the photos?

A preview arrives within 24 hours, and the full private 4K gallery, with unlimited downloads and print rights, follows in 1 to 3 business days.
