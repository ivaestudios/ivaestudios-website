Let the Kids Pick the Family Session Location

In short

Let the child pick from a parent-approved shortlist: Hilton's 2026 Trends Report found 73 percent of travelling families now involve kids in planning, and after 500 sessions we can confirm a child who chose the location behaves completely differently on camera. That ownership, not the prettiest backdrop, produces the better photographs.

Hilton's 2026 Trends Report found that 73 percent of travellers with children now involve those children in planning the trip, and 70 percent choose the destination around what the kids are interested in. Almost every article about family photo locations is written for the opposite instinct: the parent picks the prettiest backdrop and the children are placed in front of it. After 500 sessions we can tell you which approach produces better photographs, and it is not the pretty backdrop. A child who chose where to go behaves completely differently in front of a camera, and that behaviour is the photograph.

Why kids co-planning changed how we design sessions

The mechanism is simple. A child who was consulted has a stake in the outing. A child who was driven somewhere and told to smile has been interrupted. The first one runs ahead of you toward the water. The second one waits to be released. Every honest family photographer knows which of those is easier to work with, and the difference shows up in the first ninety seconds.

So we changed the intake. When a family books, we no longer just ask the parents where they would like to shoot. We ask what the children like: water, animals, climbing, boats, sand, being carried, being chased. Then we propose a location that satisfies the child's answer and the parent's aesthetic at once, which is almost always possible on this coast because the Riviera Maya has an unusual density of places that are both beautiful and genuinely fun.

What we do not do is hand the decision over entirely. A five-year-old asked to plan a photo session will choose the hotel water slide. Our job is to translate the interest into a location that also holds light at the right hour.

Six locations kids genuinely enjoy

These are the six we recommend most, and all of them have been tested repeatedly with children who did not want to be there when they got out of the car.

1. The resort beach at sunrise. Underrated because it is obvious. There is no drive, the sand is freshly cleared, the beach is empty, and a child can be back in pyjamas eating breakfast forty minutes later. If your children are small, start here.

2. Puerto Morelos town and boardwalk. A small square, painted buildings, coloured fishing boats and a leaning lighthouse, all within a two-minute walk of each other. There is ice cream. Children like the boats far more than they like the beach, and the boats photograph beautifully.

3. An open-air cenote. Cenote Cristalino near Puerto Aventuras and Cenote Azul near Playa del Carmen both have shallow shelves, stone steps and bright daylight. This is the location kids talk about afterwards. Rules are strict, life vests in deeper water and no chemical sunscreen at all, and we cover them in detail on our cenote photographer in Tulum page.

4. Akumal bay. Calm, shallow, palm-framed and famous for sea turtles. Be realistic about the turtles: swimming with them is regulated, requires a certified guide and a life vest, has no-touch rules and is not something a photographer can promise. What we can promise is a bay a child will happily stay in for an hour, and turtles are frequently visible from the shallows without going anywhere.

5. Xpu-Ha or another wide, quiet beach. Undeveloped, long and empty compared with Playa del Carmen. This is the running location. If your child's answer to what do you like was run, this is the answer.

6. The jungle edge. Green tunnels, dirt tracks and dappled light a few minutes inland. Children treat it as an expedition, which is exactly the energy we want, and it is the fallback that saves a session when the beach has sargassum on it.

Cenote, beach or jungle: choosing when the kids have opinions

If they want water and they can swim, take the cenote. It is the frame nobody else in your neighbourhood has, and children between about five and eleven find it genuinely magical. If they want water and they cannot swim yet, take the beach, and specifically a calm shallow one like Akumal or the bay side of the Hotel Zone.

If they are climbers, take the jungle or Puerto Morelos, where there is structure to be on rather than sand to be in. If they are shy, take the least populated option you can reach, because an audience is what shy children actually mind, not the camera.

And if the answer is a theme park, we have to be honest with you: the large parks on this coast generally do not permit outside professional photographers, and the ones that do treat it as a commercial shoot with paperwork attached. That is a rule set by the park, not by us. Do the park as a family day and put the photography somewhere else.

The one constraint we do not move

Whatever the children choose, the session still happens at golden hour. Midday light on this coast is brutal, and no location is good enough to fix it. If the location a child picked only works at noon, we shoot a different location and take them to that one afterwards without a camera.

Sea turtles, sand and water: sessions with an activity in them

The best family sessions we shoot have a verb in them. Not stand here, but swim, dig, climb, float, chase, paddle. When there is an activity, the children stop managing us and start ignoring us, and everything after that is real.

Digging is the most reliable of all. Give two children a stretch of wet sand and a stated goal, a hole, a channel, a wall against the tide, and they will work at it seriously for twenty minutes while we photograph the whole family around them. Water is second: floating, jumping waves, being thrown. Boats work in Puerto Morelos and the Mayakoba lagoons. Snorkel masks work anywhere, even on dry sand, because they make a child feel equipped.

What we bring is not props but permission. A lot of children arrive at a photo session believing they are supposed to keep clean. The first thing we tell them, in front of their parents so they know it is authorised, is that they are allowed to get wet. The session changes at that sentence.

Ages that need different plans

Under three: keep it to the resort beach, keep it to thirty minutes, and time it to a nap. Nothing else matters at that age. Follow them, shoot low, and accept that half the frames will be a parent's hands catching someone.

Three to five: this is negotiation age. Give two choices rather than an open question, the boats or the beach, and let them pick. They will comply with a decision they made. Bring a snack that is being saved for later.

Six to eleven: the golden window. They can swim, they can follow direction, they find it fun, and they are not yet self-conscious. This is when cenotes, longer sessions and more ambitious locations open up. If you are doing one big family session on this trip, aim it here.

Twelve and up: treat them like adults, because they are watching how you treat them. Ask them where they want to go and take the answer seriously. Give them a job, carrying something, choosing the next spot. The worst thing you can do with a teenager is arrange them.

What to promise them so the last ten minutes still work

Every family session has a decay curve, and it is steeper than parents expect. Children are excellent for roughly the first twenty-five minutes, workable to forty, and finished by fifty. The last ten minutes are where the family group portrait usually gets attempted, which is precisely the wrong order.

Two fixes. First, we shoot the formal group frames early, in the first fifteen minutes, while everyone is fresh and clean, and then spend the rest of the hour on the loose material. Parents sometimes resist this because the light is warmest at the end, but a slightly cooler frame of a happy family beats a golden frame of a tired one every time.

Second, promise them something and be specific. Not we will be quick, which children have heard before and correctly distrust, but a real, small, immediately deliverable thing: ice cream in the square afterwards, ten minutes of swimming with no camera at the end, choosing dinner. Then deliver it. We build that into the session plan and we tell the child at the start, so the last ten minutes are them finishing something rather than enduring it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should children help choose the family photo location?

Yes, within limits. A child who was consulted arrives with a stake in the outing and behaves completely differently in front of the camera. We ask what they like, water, animals, climbing, boats, and then propose a location that satisfies that and still holds good light at golden hour.

What is the best Riviera Maya location for a family session with young kids?

The resort beach at sunrise, because there is no drive, the sand is freshly cleared and the beach is empty. Puerto Morelos is our favourite alternative for its boats, boardwalk and short walking distances. Both work well for children under five.

Can we photograph a family session with sea turtles at Akumal?

Not as a promise. Swimming with turtles at Akumal is regulated, requires a certified guide and a life vest and has strict no-touch rules, so no photographer can guarantee it. Akumal bay is still an excellent session location, calm and shallow, and turtles are often visible from the shallows.

How long can children realistically last in a photo session?

About twenty-five minutes at their best, forty as a workable maximum, and most are finished by fifty. That is why we shoot the formal group portraits in the first fifteen minutes and leave the loose material for later, rather than the other way around.

Can we do the session at a theme park?

Generally no. The large parks on this coast do not usually permit outside professional photographers, and those that do treat it as a commercial shoot with paperwork. Do the park as a family day and put the photography on a beach, in a cenote or in the jungle.

What should we promise the kids to get through the session?

Something small, specific and immediate: ice cream in the square afterwards, ten minutes of swimming with no camera, or choosing dinner. Vague promises to be quick do not work because children have heard them before. We build the reward into the plan and tell them at the start.
